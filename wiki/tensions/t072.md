# T72 — Where do spacing effects come from — saturating variables inside a synapse, or a gate at the population level?

**Status:** `LEANING`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** The cheap discriminator is run: spacing effects measured across intervals full of intervening plasticity events, which defeat synaptic saturation but not a population gate.

## Position A

**Synaptic.** Multi-timescale synapse models produce the inverted-U spacing curve unaided: fast internal variables saturate when repetitions are close together, so the effective weight change is largest at an intermediate interval, and the optimal spacing is set by those variables' time constants (Benna & Fusi 2016)

## Position B

**Systems.** A non-monotonic gating function on long-term plasticity — consolidate when the fast store recalls the update at *intermediate* strength — produces the same curve, with the timescale set by population-level forgetting curves rather than by synaptic state; subdividing the slow store into sub-populations gated at different recall strengths further reproduces the observed smooth growth of retention timescale with training spacing (Lindsey & Litwin-Kumar 2024; Cepeda et al. 2006, 2008)

## Where it bites

Decides where a spaced-repetition schedule should be implemented in a machine — as a learning-rate state variable per weight, or as a write-gate on a second module — and whether spacing is a property of the substrate or a policy that can be tuned

## Status

`LEANING B` for long intervals, and the discriminator is cheap and untested: the synaptic mechanism requires that **few other plasticity events intervene** between repetitions, because interposed events prevent the saturation the effect depends on, whereas the population gate is indifferent to them. Since real inter-repetition intervals are full of intervening experience, spacing effects measured over days are evidence for B specifically. Both authors expect both mechanisms to contribute
