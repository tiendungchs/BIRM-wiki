# T352 — Should a fast store hold the *maximum* return ever seen, or an estimate of the expectation?

**Status:** `LIVE`
**Level:** `L2`
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]
**Closes when:** One episodic-control agent, one benchmark, one embedding, with the write rule as the only variable: `max{Q, R_t}` against a tabular update `Q ← Q + α(R_t − Q)`, run on a matched pair of environments differing only in transition/reward noise (deterministic and a stochastic variant of the same task). The row resolves toward A if the `max` rule's advantage on the deterministic arm exceeds its loss on the stochastic arm at every noise level tested, and toward B if the crossing is early.

## Position A

**Keep the maximum: the store's job is to replay a success, not to estimate a distribution.** Blundell et al. 2016 ([[wiki/entities/model-free-episodic-control.md]]) write `Q^EC(s,a) ← R_t` on first sight and `max{Q^EC(s,a), R_t}` thereafter, and state the reading: the stored value is an estimate of the *highest potential return*, not the expected one. The argument is that near-deterministic transitions and rewards are ubiquitous in natural environments — leave a room and return and you are back where you started — and that an algorithm built for the general stochastic case pays for that generality in samples it need not spend. The measured consequence is one-shot capture: on Labyrinth's Double-T-Maze, a single lucky trajectory is enough for a positive-reward policy where A3C never exceeds zero at any budget. [[wiki/entities/hami.md]] adopts the same rule independently, on a quantised key, without stating the assumption.

## Position B

**Track the expectation: a store that can only increase cannot be corrected.** [[wiki/entities/neural-episodic-control.md]] replaces the `max` with a tabular Q-learning update at a large learning rate `α` on `N`-step returns, so a stored value can move down as well as up, and beats MFEC by roughly 20 median human-normalised points at 10M Atari frames. The general form of the objection is the one Position A's own paper concedes: since the stored value can never decrease, the rule "is not suited to rational action selection in stochastic environments" — an optimistic bias with no correction path, which in a noisy environment converges to the best outcome ever observed and then acts on it forever. The `max` also destroys the information needed to know it is wrong: a single lucky return and fifty consistent ones are indistinguishable in the stored scalar.

## Where it bites

On what an inter-module edge out of the fast store is allowed to carry, which is a signal-flow question and not a tuning one. Under A the store exports a *bound* — "this much was achievable from here" — and any downstream arbiter reading it must supply its own confidence from elsewhere, because the store has architecturally discarded the spread. Under B the store exports a point estimate of a mean and the spread is recoverable in principle by instrumenting the update. Every arbitration proposal in the wiki assumes the latter: [[wiki/concepts/complementary-learning-systems.md]]'s "when to trust the fast system", the uncertainty-based competition MFEC's own discussion endorses, and the confidence read [[wiki/concepts/retrieval-capacity.md]] and `G42` ask every store to provide all require a quantity the `max` rule deletes at write time. So the two positions are not two hyperparameters; they decide whether a fast store can participate in arbitration at all.

## Status

`LIVE`, and the head-to-head that would settle it has never been run. The two primary sources differ in the write rule *and* in the embedding (fixed vs TD-learned), the read (unweighted `k`-NN vs kernel-weighted), and the return (Monte Carlo vs `N`-step), so NEC's 20-point margin over MFEC is not attributable to the write rule at all — it is confounded three ways, and the paper's own decomposition credits the learned embedding. Both benchmarks used are deterministic, so no evidence in the wiki exercises the regime where the positions actually disagree. The cheapest discriminator is the stochastic arm, which no episodic-control paper here has ever run.
