# T191 — Should a solver commit to one parse of the input, or carry several?

**Status:** `LIVE`  
**Level:** `L1`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** The unreported quantity is reported: how often the top-ranked parse is the one the eventual solution needed — and the two parse policies are compared on one task set with the parse policy the only variable.

## Position A

**Commit — but choose which, by a computed score.** [[wiki/entities/arc-vsa-solver.md]] holds six object hypotheses, ranks them by one number (mean over output objects of the max softmax of their similarities to candidate input objects — penalising both too many objects and ambiguous correspondence), and tries them in order, keeping the first whose implied action set is cheap enough. One parse is live at a time; the alternatives are never composed. The argument is cognitive and computational at once: the parse space is smaller than the action space, so search it first, and System 1 proposes a parse before System 2 examines any transformation

## Position B

**Do not commit.** [[wiki/entities/ilp-arc-synthesizer.md]] represents a grid as a list of *possibly-overlapping* objects — a rectangle is also lines is also points — and carries all readings forward until the relation that explains the transformation selects its own endpoints; the parse is chosen after the fact by downstream explanatory success. [[wiki/entities/corethink-compositional-reasoner.md]] takes the third position by not asking: one fixed 8-connected BFS parse, with a frozen LLM patching ambiguous cases

## Where it bites

Decides where the object-hypothesis cost is paid and what a wrong parse costs. Under A a parse error is fatal and unrecoverable (the ranking is computed on demonstrations and cannot be checked against the query at all), but each candidate is cheap and the criterion is inspectable; under B nothing is ever wrong but the hypothesis space is multiplied by the parse ambiguity and typing is doing the pruning. It is G27 in its practical form: the wiki has been asking where a discretisation comes from and has never asked whether exactly one is needed

## Status

`LIVE` — no experiment compares them: A's six-hypothesis menu and B's typed superset are evaluated on different task sets (400+400 vs 5 hand-picked), and neither reports an ablation in which the parse policy is the only thing varied. **A quantity that would decide it exists and is unreported**: how often A's top-ranked parse is the one its own eventual solution needed

**A third stance the row does not have a column for** ([[wiki/concepts/visual-routines.md]], Ullman 1984): *carry none*. Both positions assume a parse is a thing a solver holds — one of them, or all of them — between seeing the input and answering. Ullman's alternative is that the parse is never represented at all: a spatial relation is computed by a routine assembled for the question, and the only thing retained is whatever that routine wrote into an incremental representation, keyed to the question that ran it. Under this stance A's "parse error is fatal" and B's "hypothesis space multiplied" are both costs of a commitment nobody has to make, and the cost moves to assembly plus recomputation — the same relation asked twice about the same input is computed twice, unless the incremental representation happens to hold the intermediate. It is unimplemented and so cannot be scored against A or B, but it changes what the row's experiment should measure: not which parse policy wins, but whether *the retained structure differs when the same input is asked two different questions*, which no solver in the wiki is even capable of exhibiting.

**Sibling row (S12 audit, lint 23): T306** asks the same commit-or-carry question one stage later — at the mapping rather than the parse — where the enumerator is SME and the committers are ACME, LISA and the APNN `argmax`. Neither row's experiment has been run, and the two ambiguities multiply: a solver that carries `k` parses into a matcher that carries `m` interpretations pays `k·m`.
