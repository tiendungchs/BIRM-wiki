# T396 — In a common currency, are a gain and a loss of the same magnitude equal and opposite?

**Status:** `LIVE`  
**Level:** `L0`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** The generalized-matching bias parameter is estimated for token gain against token loss in the **same subjects, same currency, same schedule family**, with reinforcement density controlled by a yoked condition (as in Raiff et al. 2008) and with the loss delivered both as response-contingent removal and as avoidable removal. Position A predicts bias ≈ 0 with sensitivity unchanged; Position B predicts a bias of the order of 3 : 1 toward the unpunished alternative. Running it in a non-human species under the same token procedure decides the cross-species half.

## Position A

**Symmetric: punishment is a primary process, and losses and gains are changes of sign along one dimension.** This is the *symmetrical law of effect*, assumed in every major quantitative theory of behaviour, and token procedures are what make it testable — gains and losses denominated in one unit remove the scaling problem that blocks comparing food to shock. Critchfield, Paletz, MacAleese & Newland 2003 pitted a **direct suppression** model (punishment primary, symmetric) against a **competitive suppression** model (punishment secondary, asymmetric) on human concurrent schedules of point gain and loss: in 17 of 20 cases where the models diverged, direct suppression fitted better. Magoon & Critchfield 2008 obtained matching functions for positive reinforcement (point gain) and negative reinforcement (point-loss avoidance) of equal magnitude in the same subjects and found **no bias** — equal impact per consequence type.

## Position B

**Asymmetric: a loss weighs more than the corresponding gain.** Rasmussen & Newland 2008, human concurrent schedules with the generalized matching law: superimposing contingent point loss on one alternative sharply reduced sensitivity and produced a strong bias toward the unpunished side, larger than relative reinforcement rates predict — estimated at roughly **3 : 1**, a loss worth about three gains. Prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky 1979) makes the same claim as an axiom: the value function is steeper for losses. In non-humans, Chen, Lakshminarayanan & Santos 2006 had capuchins spend a 12-token budget across a gain-framed and a loss-framed option with **identical returns** (1.5 items/trial) and found preference for the gain frame; Brosnan et al. 2007 report a status-quo/endowment effect in chimpanzees.

## Where it bites

**Whether one signed scalar channel can carry a manufactured reward and its withdrawal.** Under A, an intrinsic-reward system can credit and debit on one wire with one gain term — withdrawing `r̃` is exactly negating it, and an agent's balance is a signed sum. Under B, debiting requires its own weight, the exchange rate is not 1, and every quantity accumulated from mixed gains and losses depends on the order and framing of its components — which is the shape of the reference-point machinery on [[wiki/concepts/affective-opponency.md]] (a movable valence origin) appearing inside a *learned currency* rather than inside primary valence.

It also bites on `G127` directly. If manufactured reward is held as a balance with its own redemption schedule, the wiki needs a debit operation for it; A makes that free and B makes it a second learned parameter with its own dynamics.

What each side **denies**. A cannot express the capuchin framing result or Rasmussen & Newland's bias magnitude without attributing them to uncontrolled reinforcement-density changes — which is precisely the confound Raiff et al. 2008's yoked control was built to remove, and it removed it in the *direction of a real punishment effect* without settling its size. B cannot explain Magoon & Critchfield's null bias in the same species with the same currency, nor Critchfield et al.'s 17-of-20 model comparison. Silberberg et al. 2008 offer a non-loss-aversion reading of the capuchin data, so B's only non-human support is itself contested.

Method asymmetry: A's evidence comes from model-comparison and matching-bias estimates under *symmetrically arranged* schedules; B's comes from experiments where the loss contingency is superimposed on one alternative, i.e. where the punished option also loses reinforcement density. No experiment in the source combines the token currency, the yoked density control and the matching-bias estimate — which is exactly what the closing condition asks for.
