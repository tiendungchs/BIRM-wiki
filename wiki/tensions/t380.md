# T380 — Is the prediction-error cohort a sparse dedicated subpopulation, or a threshold slice through a variable every superficial cell carries?

**Status:** `LIVE`  
**Level:** `L0-INSTR`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** The same L2/3 cells are read simultaneously by a calcium indicator and by a spike- or voltage-faithful measure (cell-attached, or a genetically encoded voltage indicator) during closed-loop mismatch, and the reported "mismatch-responsive fraction" is plotted as a function of the detection threshold. A holds if the calcium-positive cells are a stable, re-identifiable cohort whose subthreshold neighbours show no mismatch modulation. B holds if the fraction slides continuously with threshold and the calcium-positive set is the tail of a distribution with no gap in it.

## Position A

**A sparse dedicated cohort.** Two-photon calcium imaging in mouse V1 reports that **20–30%** of L2/3 neurons respond to visuomotor mismatch — a fraction repeatedly recovered across laboratories and larger than the fraction responding to any one natural scene (3%) or one drifting grating (12%), which is the standard argument that mismatch is a *dedicated* channel rather than an incidental modulation (Keller et al. 2012; Zmarz & Keller 2016; Attinger et al. 2017, [[wiki/concepts/prediction-error-neurons.md]]). Under this reading the prediction-error neuron is a cell type: the wiki's whole cell-class decomposition, and every silencing or photostimulation experiment designed against it, presupposes a population one can target.

## Position B

**A rectified tail of a dense graded variable.** Whole-cell recording in the same preparation finds **78%** (25/32) of L2/3 excitatory cells with a significant subthreshold mismatch response, distributed **unimodally** from strongly hyperpolarizing to strongly depolarizing, while only **10%** of the same cells emit a spiking mismatch response (Jordan & Keller 2020, [[wiki/concepts/prediction-error-neurons.md]]). Neither number matches the imaging figure: the subthreshold population is three times larger, the spiking population two to three times smaller. The membrane-potential variable is continuous and near-universal; "a mismatch neuron" is then a labelling of whichever cells happened to clear the threshold of whichever instrument was used, and the 20–30% is an artefact of calcium's own transfer function.

## Where it bites

**Whether "prediction-error neuron" names a part of the decomposition or a measurement outcome.** Under A the error channel is a distinct block with a cell count, a targetable identity and — per `T341` — two sign-specific variants; a machine layer can allocate dedicated error units. Under B every superficial unit computes a signed residual all of the time and the sparse spiking is a *transmission* policy, so the right machine analogue is a dense signed layer with a rectifying output nonlinearity and a low firing threshold, and any experiment that opto-tags "the mismatch cells" has tagged the top decile of a continuum and will mis-attribute the effect of silencing it. It also decides how to read every population fraction in the wiki's mouse-V1 evidence base, including the feature-specificity argument, which counts cells.

Two reconciling factors are named by the source itself and neither has been tested: intracellular dialysis and the higher mismatch presentation rate used during whole-cell recording could both suppress spiking, and calcium rises independent of spiking would inflate the imaging fraction. Until one is measured, the three numbers (78 / 20–30 / 10) cannot be placed on one axis.

## Status

`LIVE`. Cheap to move and never attempted: the discrepancy is between two instruments applied to the same cell type in the same laboratory's own preparation, so the controlled comparison needs no new animal model. It is filed `L0-INSTR` rather than `L1` because the decomposition question it feeds — how many error units a cortical module has — is already `T341`'s; this row is about whether the counting instrument supports any answer at all.
