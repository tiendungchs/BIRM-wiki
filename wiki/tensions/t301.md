# T301 — Did the human cortex scale in units or in wiring-per-unit?

**Status:** `LIVE`  
**Level:** `L4`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** A whole-brain isotropic-fractionator count of a chimpanzee (or any great ape) cerebral cortex is published and compared against the human count by the same method, so that the human:chimpanzee neuron ratio is measured rather than inferred from one 1953 density study or from a regression fitted without apes.

## Position A

**Wiring-per-unit. The tripling is neuropil, not neurons.** Sherwood, Subiaul & Zawidzki 2008 ([[wiki/concepts/emergent-modularity.md]]), citing Shariff 1953: human cerebral cortex is **2.75× chimpanzee volume with 1.25× the neurons**. Corroborating measurements point the same way — the glia:neuron ratio is the highest of any primate and rises with brain size; the excess temporal-lobe white matter sits in **gyral** rather than core white matter, i.e. local connectivity between neighbouring fields; humans have as much eulaminate cortex as predicted and slightly *fewer* eulaminate neurons. This is the whole basis of `G101`, and of the source's own mechanism for generalised systematicity (afferent diversity into a fixed composition site — a fan-in claim). **Second-hand support at cell level** (Galakhova et al. 2022, [[wiki/entities/human-supragranular-pyramidal-neuron.md]]): across mammals "including primates", cortical thickness rises and neuron density falls with brain size (DeFelipe 2011; Balaram & Kaas 2014; Semendeferi et al. 2011), while synapse density stays roughly constant, so synapses per neuron are highest in humans (Elston et al. 2001; Sherwood et al. 2020); human L2/3 cells carry ~2× the synapses of rodent cells.

## Position B

**Units, at constant wiring-per-unit.** Herculano-Houzel 2009 ([[wiki/concepts/cellular-scaling-rules.md]]): across primates measured by isotropic fractionation, cortical mass scales as `M_CX ∼ N_CX^1.077` and neuronal density and the non-neuronal/neuronal ratio show **no systematic change** with brain size. Under that exponent 2.75× cortical volume is ~2.5× the neurons, not 1.25×. The rule set predicts the human whole-brain count to within 7% (93 B expected, 86 B measured) while being fitted *without* human values, and partial cerebellar counts in orangutan and gorilla fall on the same lines. A>1 scaling — mass growing faster than units, hence `s̄` growing — is the **rodent** regime (`N^1.744` in cortex), which is exactly the one primates are measured not to be in.

## Where it bites

`G101` in full, and the wiki's choice of scaling axis. If A, the one biological scale-up that produced a qualitative change happened on an axis no wiki architecture has ever moved, and unit-count scaling is the wrong lever. If B, `G101`'s premise is an artefact and the comparative record instead says the opposite and more actionable thing — that the lineage with the better cognition-per-gram is the one that held wiring-per-unit **fixed** while adding units, which is the sparse-fixed-fan-in regime and a claim about exponents rather than about efficiency at a size. The two are not yet a formal contradiction: A's ratio comes from a single 1953 study that multiplied regional density estimates by volume, the method Herculano-Houzel's whole-brain homogenate counts were built to replace, and whose errors on whale and elephant cortex the same source documents; B's primate regression contains **no great apes at all** in its cortical fit, so a hominid-specific departure from the primate exponent is compatible with every number in it. Note also that the two positions are not about the same quantity if the comparison species differ in cortical *area* versus *thickness* — neither source separates them.

## Status

`LIVE`. Galakhova et al. 2022 cites **both** positions without reconciling them: Herculano-Houzel 2012 for "the human brain has the number of neurons expected for a primate brain of its size" (B), and a falling-density literature "including primates" for A. The two fit together only if the falling density comes from non-primate orders, from specific areas (Semendeferi et al. 2011 is frontal pole), or from thickness rather than area — the split this row already notes neither side makes. Sherwood et al. 2020, the review's primate synapse-density source, was not acquired (excluded venue).
