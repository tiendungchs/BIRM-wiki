# T398 — Is the subjective cost of mental effort convex in effort, or concave (hyperbolic)?

**Status:** `LIVE`  
**Level:** `L0`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** Effort risk preferences and effort discount curves are elicited from the *same* subjects on the *same* demand axis with the unit of effort fixed by construction (equal duration, equal payment contingency, load as the only varying quantity). A convex cost predicts risk aversion over effort gambles *and* a discount curve whose increments grow; a concave cost predicts a decelerating discount curve *and* risk **seeking** over effort. Any result in which one subject shows both risk aversion and a decelerating curve refutes the assumption that one function generates both — which would close the row as ill-posed rather than for either side.

## Position A

**Convex.** People are risk-averse over effort: offered a certain amount of effort against a gamble with the same expected effort, they take the certain one (Apps et al. 2015). Under the standard behavioural-economic reading (the same argument that makes utility of money concave from risk aversion over money), risk aversion over a cost implies a **convex** cost function — successive increments of effort hurt more and more.

## Position B

**Concave (hyperbolic).** Fitting candidate functions to effort-discounting choices, the data are best described by a **hyperbolic**, hence concave, cost — the same functional family as delay discounting (Chong et al. 2017). Successive increments of effort hurt less and less.

## Where it bites

**Whether an agent can ever decline a task outright.** A hyperbolic discount *can never fully erase* the value of a prospective payoff — the discounted value approaches zero asymptotically and stays positive — so under Position B there is no finite demand level at which an agent refuses both the effort and the payoff. Yet complete disengagement is the modal observation (Kool & Botvinick 2018 raise exactly this objection to B). A convex cost crosses zero net value at a finite load and produces refusal for free, but predicts an interior optimum with sharply escalating marginal cost that the discounting data do not show.

For an architecture, the shape is not cosmetic: it fixes whether the control-intensity optimum of [[wiki/concepts/expected-value-of-control.md]] is interior, whether a demand ceiling exists, and whether effort and delay can share one kernel ([[wiki/concepts/subjective-value.md]]) or need two.

## Status

`LIVE`, and the source's own diagnosis is that it may be **unresolvable from choice behaviour alone**. Inferring a cost function from choices requires quantifying effort in a fixed unit, but effort is not a property of the task: it depends jointly on the individual's cognitive capacity and on how much effort they voluntarily mobilise, which is itself a function of their reward sensitivity. Kool & Botvinick name this the **econometric problem in mental effort research**. Both positions above fit choice data with an unidentified `x`-axis, so the two curves may not be disagreeing about the same function. This makes the row `L0-INSTR`-flavoured in its resolution even though the claim in dispute is `L0`: the discriminating design must fix the effort unit by construction (as cognitive-effort discounting fixes duration and payment contingency) before either fit means anything.

A third quantity is settled and must not be mistaken for a resolution: the **net utility surface** over income and leisure is concave (Kool & Botvinick 2014, the income-compensated wage test — [[wiki/concepts/effort-based-decision-making.md]]). That is the sum of a benefit and a cost, not the cost, and it constrains the pair rather than either term.

A fourth candidate sidesteps the axis entirely: if the cost is a **divergence from a default policy** ([[wiki/concepts/default-policy-regularization.md]]) its argument is distance-from-habit rather than demand, and neither fitted curve is being computed over the right variable.
