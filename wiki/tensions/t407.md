# T407 — Is recognition familiarity a read of the episodic store, or a separate module that survives the store's removal?

**Status:** `LIVE`  
**Level:** `L1`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]]  
**Closes when:** A lesion confined to the hippocampal formation (subiculum included, histologically verified, made without ischaemia) is tested in the same subjects on item recognition and associative recognition, and familiarity and recollection are each estimated with a bias-corrected method (ROC or equivalent). A holds if the familiarity estimate falls roughly in proportion to recollection. B holds if familiarity stays at control level while recollection falls. The test must also include the reverse case: a perirhinal-restricted lesion that lowers familiarity while recollection stays at control level.

## Position A

**One store, read two ways.** Familiarity is a property of the same declarative store that supports recall: the strength of the item's match against stored traces, before any context is returned. Zola & Squire (commentary on Aggleton & Brown 1999, [[wiki/concepts/recollection-familiarity.md]]) report recognition deficits in 6 of 7 monkey studies with lesions limited to the hippocampal region. Patients with CA1-only damage (RB, GD) are impaired on recognition, and impairment grows with the number of medial-temporal structures damaged. Knowlton argues that "know" is redundant with "remember": over a week, 29% of items move from remember to know and 10% the other way. Pickering finds that a familiarity-only model fits healthy yes/no ROCs better than a dual-process model. Brown & Aggleton 2001 ([[wiki/entities/perirhinal-cortex.md]]), though from camp B, report two more A-shaped results: hypoxic hippocampal cases impaired across many recognition tests (Reed & Squire 1997; Manns & Squire), and hippocampus-centred amnesics impaired on both remember and know (Knowlton & Squire 1995). They concede the second favours a single process. The wiki's own instances take this shape too. [[wiki/concepts/key-value-memory.md]] reads familiarity as the store's key match, and [[wiki/entities/esbn.md]] gets the split from ablating one scalar inside one memory.

## Position B

**Two modules.** Familiarity is computed in perirhinal cortex, from a one-trial, stimulus-specific repetition decrement that is rare in the hippocampus. It survives removal of the hippocampal–fornix–anterior-thalamic system (Aggleton & Brown 1999, [[wiki/concepts/recollection-familiarity.md]]). The evidence:

- Rat lesion double dissociations between object recognition and allocentric spatial memory, including a within-subject version (Glenn & Mumby 1996).
- c-*fos*: item novelty engages perirhinal cortex and not the hippocampus; context novelty engages the hippocampus.
- Normal Recognition Memory Test scores in hypoxic-hippocampal, fornix and selective diencephalic groups.
- Neurotoxic monkey hippocampal lesions with no deficit at 40-min delays.
- Yonelinas et al.'s bias-corrected re-analysis: amnesics lose recollection disproportionately.
- Hintzman: recognition and frequency judgements become available ~100 ms before list, modality or location judgements.
- Brown & Aggleton 2001 ([[wiki/entities/perirhinal-cortex.md]]; same lab, not independent): the perirhinal decrement is readable at ~75 ms, as fast as identity, which they take to exclude top-down hippocampal input. Repetition effects earlier in the visual pathway last only seconds, so the >24 h signal starts in perirhinal cortex/TE.
- Event-related fMRI (Eldridge et al. 2000): hippocampal activity rises only for "remember". "Know" is indistinguishable from correct rejections and misses, so in the hippocampus familiarity is not a weaker recollection.
- **The reverse case** (Bowles et al. 2007, [[wiki/entities/perirhinal-cortex.md]]; Köhler lab with Yonelinas, independent of the Aggleton lab). Patient NB had a left anterior temporal resection that took part of perirhinal and entorhinal cortex and spared the hippocampus. Recollection was normal or above and familiarity impaired, across remember/know (twice), bias-corrected ROC and a 400 vs 2,000 ms response deadline. Her ROC became more linear in probability space and more U-shaped in z-space. No single-strength model predicts that shape, and a response-bias shift cannot produce it. The authors' reading: the hippocampus does not need an intact familiarity signal as input to recollect.

## A third reading: the split is by attribute

Kesner, Knowlton and Mumby (commentaries) read the dissociation as a split of **content type**, not of process. The hippocampus does both familiarity and recollection for spatial information, and perirhinal cortex does both for objects. On this reading the lesion data are real, but "familiarity module" is the wrong label. A&B's reply: combined perirhinal + postrhinal lesions spare spatial memory although that cortex receives spatial input, and the two regions' cytoarchitecture differs (convergent columns versus a divergent hippocampus), so they process differently, not just different inputs. Bowles et al. 2007 hold content fixed (neutral words throughout) and still separate the two processes with a perirhinal/entorhinal lesion, which the attribute reading does not predict.

## Where it bites

**Whether a reasoning architecture's "have I seen this?" gate needs its own module with its own item code.**

| | Under A | Under B |
|---|---|---|
| Where the gate lives | In the store, as the pre-normalisation key match | In a separate encoder-side habituation trace |
| When the store fails | The gate fails with it | The gate survives and can report the failure (`G42`'s silent-failure complaint) |
| Cost | One scalar per head | A second learned population with one-shot, long-lasting, item-specific plasticity |
| What the gate can see | Anything the store bound, including arrangements | Single items only. Novel arrangements of familiar items are invisible to it (Honey et al. 1998) |

B also turns the novelty signal into **two signals**: item novelty from the gate and arrangement novelty from the store. A single prediction-error novelty term would then conflate two triggers that call for different writes.

## Status

`LIVE`. The 1999 debate ends with both camps holding their data. A's monkey evidence is mostly ischaemic or radio-frequency lesions, which B attributes to hidden pathology. B's human evidence is mostly single cases and group means on one test (the Recognition Memory Test), and nobody has shown that test to be familiarity-only. Both sides agree that most amnesics lose both signals. The decisive comparison (selective lesion, both processes estimated with bias correction, same subjects) is what the commentators ask for and no one in the source reports. Brown & Aggleton 2001 sort the evidence by class: activity in the intact brain is **qualitatively** dissociated (units, c-*fos*, fMRI, event-related potentials), animal lesion behaviour only **quantitatively** per lesion, and human lesion behaviour is **equivocal**. In their words, "the behavioural case for dissociation remains to be proved". They add no selective lesion with bias-corrected estimates. They also give no reverse case (perirhinal damage, familiarity lost, recollection kept).

Bowles et al. 2007 supply the reverse case, with bias correction, and it favours B. The row stays `LIVE` because neither half of `Closes when` is met:

- **Reverse half, partly met.** The lesion is not perirhinal-restricted: it also takes amygdala, temporal pole and part of entorhinal cortex. It is partial and unilateral, and there is one subject. The corrected familiarity estimates are borderline in two of three experiments (P < 0.07, P = 0.056). The left hippocampus is mildly atrophic. The authors concede that a larger perirhinal or entorhinal lesion might still impair hippocampal function.
- **Hippocampal half, unmet.** No histologically verified hippocampal lesion has item and associative recognition estimated with bias correction in the same subjects.
