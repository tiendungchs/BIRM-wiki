# G68 — Every architecture in the wiki is a proposer; nothing is a rejector

**Status:** `OPEN`  
**Kind:** `part`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/architectural-gaps.md]]  
**Closes when:** A rejector whose correctness criterion is independent of the generator is attached to a wiki architecture, and lifts output quality past the point where the generator's own confidence stops predicting correctness.

## Why it blocks the target

A generator's ceiling is the point at which its output quality stops being distinguishable from its fluency, and the only thing that has ever lifted it in a domain with a mechanical acceptance test is an external check whose correctness criterion is independent of the generator's training objective (Raiyan et al. 2026). The wiki holds program inducers, amortised recognition nets, planners, and joint-embedding encoders — all of which *emit* a candidate — and no component that decides which candidates survive, at what cost, and with how many bits returned per rejection. The three conditions the role requires (accurate, resistant to exploitation by the generator, informative on partial progress) also rule out the obvious internal candidates. **A second internal case, this one at the level of whole answers** ([[wiki/entities/corethink-compositional-reasoner.md]]): a meta-classifier picking 1 of 4 candidate ARC grids produced by two different pipelines is worth +4.2 points over the best single solver (26.6 → 30.8), while generating nothing and executing nothing — and the selector is the same model family that produced half the candidates, i.e. the agent-consensus rung with the shared-misconception channel open. The authors attribute the gain to *generator diversity* rather than selector quality and run no control (random selection among the four) that would separate the two. The same source supplies the row's cleanest positive result, which is not a rejector at all but a relocation: counting agreement over **hypothesis labels across demonstrations** is worth 2.2× what counting agreement over **answer grids across samples** is worth, at additive rather than multiplicative cost.

## Best current answer

None that is external. [[wiki/concepts/simulation-based-planning.md]] rejects a plan whose rollout misses, but the test is computed by the same learned model it checks — and the wiki already records that a planner optimising through a learned model finds and exploits the model's spurious structure. [[wiki/concepts/violation-of-expectation.md]] is the outcome rung: one bit, at the end. [[wiki/concepts/precision-weighting.md]] scores partial progress but is a learned confidence emitted by the generative model itself, i.e. the Goodhart row of the failure table. The only externality the biology plainly has is the environment, consulted by acting, which ties this row to the passive/active split ([[wiki/concepts/universal-induction.md]])  **The row now has a price for the internal case, which sharpens rather than fills it** ([[wiki/entities/autotom.md]]): dropping latent-variable hypotheses whose local conditional is low (`P(o|s) = 0.01`) before the joint is assembled is worth +1.28 accuracy at −35% tokens and −38% API calls across five benchmarks — strictly dominant, and computed by the same model that proposed the hypotheses. So an internal rejector is not worthless; it is a *pruner* that pays for itself on compute and cannot lift the ceiling, which is the distinction this row was collapsing. What lifts a ceiling has to be independent of the proposer; what pays for itself need not be. **The largest single price the wiki has for this role, and it is external to the model** ([[wiki/concepts/refinement-loop.md]]): an application-layer **refinement harness** takes a frozen Gemini 3 Pro from 31% to **54%** on ARC-AGI-2 by wrapping it in a propose-check-retry loop, at 38× the cost per task ($0.81 → $31); the same harness gets comparable gains on Claude Opus 4.5. +23 points from code that does not touch the weights is the cleanest evidence in the wiki that the rejector, not the proposer, is the binding constraint — with the caveat that the observed harnesses are domain-specific and the report expects them to be absorbed behind vendor APIs, which would make the proposer/rejector boundary invisible in every future reported score ([[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]] T206). **The row's premise measured inside a single system, which is what it had been missing** ([[wiki/entities/poe-arc-solver.md]]): holding generator, selector and augmentation set fixed and only lowering the DFS acceptance threshold from `T=9%` to `T=0.5%` raises the fraction of ARC-AGI-1 tasks whose candidate set *contains* the correct answer by 7.5 points (76.0 → 83.5) and the final score by **0.2** (71.6 → 71.8), at 6.5× runtime — so 11.7 points of the system's ceiling sit in the generate/select gap and every further point of proposer quality is wasted. The same source supplies the row's first *proof* that a rejector helps and by how much (`KL(P‖P̄) = mean_j KL(P‖P̂_j) + log Z`, `log Z ≤ 0`, so pooling beats a random single view by exactly the amount the views disagree), the first measured aggregator ordering (`∏` 71.6 > `min` 68.8 > mean 66.6 > `max` 63.5 on one candidate set — veto rules beat averaging rules, and trusting the most confident view is worst), and the sharpest statement of the row's limit: the *same* rejector selects the correct answer 100% of the time on Sudoku and ~86% on ARC, so a checker's power is a property of the domain's local checkability and not of the checker. **The row's origin has a price, and it is not the one the row assumes** ([[wiki/entities/gsm8k.md]], Cobbe et al. 2021 — the paper that introduced the pairing): a generator plus a *separately trained* verifier reranking 100 samples was worth roughly a **30× parameter increase**, and the winning configuration is a **large generator with a small verifier**, not the reverse — so the missing component is the *cheap* half, and the authors read the asymmetry as the verifier applying coarse heuristics rather than verifying. Two bounds come with it: verification is *worse* than no verification below a dataset-size threshold (the checker overfits to the correct answer before it learns properties of correct reasoning), and the search it enables has a **maximum** — accuracy rises to ~400 ranked candidates and then falls as the search finds solutions that fool the scorer ([[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]] T220). **And the row's extreme case, which is a scale sweep rather than an ablation** ([[wiki/entities/olymmath.md]]): on OlymMATH-HARD a 1.5B model reaches **Pass@64 = 30.0% with Cons@64 = 0.0%** — every problem it can solve at least once is solved by a minority of its own samples, so the plurality is always wrong and the vote rung has *negative* value. At 7B and 32B the same benchmark shows Pass@64 tied at 74.0 / 75.0 while Pass@1 differs 1.5×, so 4.5× the parameters buy a sampling rate rather than new reachable problems. The rejector is not a refinement on a good proposer here; it is the entire difference between 30% and 0%. **And the row's framing is now split in two, because one half of it is solved** ([[wiki/concepts/selective-prediction.md]], Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017): an *internal* rejector cannot lift the proposer's ceiling — this row's premise — and does not have to, because at fixed `f` a threshold on the model's own max-softmax moves selective risk to **any** target by moving coverage, with a distribution-free bound (1% risk at 78.6% coverage on CIFAR-10, 2% top-5 risk at 59.4% coverage on ImageNet, `δ=0.001`). So the two roles the row was collapsing are: an internal `κ_f` buys a **trade with a proof**, an external verifier buys a **ceiling lift without one**, and they are complements rather than competitors. The catch for this row is that the trade is only demonstrated where the label set is closed; OlymMATH's `C@64 = 0.0` is the same mechanism with an anti-monotone `κ_f` (`T320`). **And the row's cost premise is now known to be benchmark-specific** ([[wiki/entities/prm800k.md]], Lightman et al. 2023): the reason the wiki prices a step-level rejector as expensive and an outcome-level one as cheap is that MATH has a machine-checkable final answer. Where it does not, establishing outcome correctness *is* the act of finding the first mistake, so the two cost the same and the cheap rung has no reason to exist — which removes the standing excuse for attaching an outcome-only rejector to a wiki architecture, since every domain here (perception, planning, world modelling) is on the wrong side of that line. The same source gives the row its first *data-collection* rule: label where two rungs disagree (the learned scorer rates the candidate highly ∧ an independent check says it is wrong), worth ≈2.6× data efficiency with no uncertainty estimate, ensemble or gradient — plus two bounds, that the multiplier decays once the selected fraction of the candidate pool is large, and that closing the loop by retraining the selector during collection was unstable and gave no gain.
