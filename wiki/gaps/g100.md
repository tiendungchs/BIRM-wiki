# G100 — No architecture in the wiki has a growth schedule: capacity is fully available at step 0, so nothing can arrive already conditioned on what an earlier, smaller version of itself learned

**Status:** `PARTIAL`  
**Kind:** `part`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/architectural-gaps.md]]  
**Closes when:** An architecture grows capacity on a schedule, so that later capacity arrives conditioned on what a smaller earlier version learned, and the grown model beats a full-size-from-step-0 twin at matched final size.

## Why it blocks the target

The one lever Sherwood et al. 2008 give the most weight is developmental timing, and it has the sharpest numbers in the source: the human neonatal brain is **~27%** of adult size against **36%** in chimpanzees and **70%** in macaques, and postnatal growth continues at the *fetal* rate through the whole first year — so the majority of human wiring is laid down while socially embedded, and triadic joint attention (the first unambiguously human item in the behavioural subtraction) appears at ~9 months, inside that window. The claim is that *when* capacity becomes available is itself a structural prior, because late capacity is conditioned on what early capacity already learned. Every model in the wiki violates this: parameter count, depth and module inventory are fixed before the first gradient step, so no representation in any of them was ever formed under a capacity constraint it later escaped. **Somel et al. 2009 specifies the shape the schedule has, and it is not a global slowdown.** Fitting a scalar age-warp `Δ` per gene against a trajectory whose shape is conserved from mouse to human (median `r = 0.83`), the human-specific delay is carried by **~4% of the prefrontal transcriptome**, is over-represented among **gray-matter**-specific genes and absent from white-matter ones (`P = 0.0001` vs `P > 0.6`), and peaks at **~10 years** — the synaptic-elimination window. A transcriptome-wide shift is positively excluded by simulation. So the object `G100` asks for is a **sparse vector of per-module phase offsets over a shared curve**, applied to the operator that *commits* capacity rather than to the learning rate — one scalar per module, cheap enough to be learnable ([[wiki/concepts/developmental-heterochrony.md]])

## Best current answer

**Nothing directly.** Three adjacent things exist and none is this. (i) `G32` is the *data* schedule — nothing designs the experience stream — which is the complementary half and is separately open; a curriculum changes the input while capacity stays fixed. (ii) [[wiki/concepts/continual-learning.md]] schedules *plasticity* (importance-gated writes) over a network whose size never changes. (iii) [[wiki/concepts/complementary-learning-systems.md]] has two components with different rates, but both exist from the start. The biological version is not a rate difference: it is a component that is **absent, then present, and whose absence shaped what the present components hold**. The source's own control condition for the lever is enculturated apes — change the early environment, get a different repertoire, in an animal whose genome did not move — which is `G1`'s factorization demonstrated within a lifetime and from the environment side. **One thing is now available and unused: the test.** Somel et al.'s heterochrony test — fit `f_A(t) = f_B(t + Δ)` against a constant-offset null by `F`-test, with a third system as outgroup to give `Δ` a polarity — runs on any pair of checkpointed training trajectories with per-unit or per-probe curves in place of per-gene ones, and would say whether two architectures differ in schedule or in mechanism. Nothing in the wiki has fitted one (`DH5`) **The row's first architecture with an actual growth schedule, and its first matched-size control** (Rusu et al. 2016, [[wiki/entities/progressive-neural-networks.md]]): capacity is added one frozen column at a time, each new column reading the earlier ones' features at every depth, so later capacity does arrive conditioned on what the earlier, smaller version learned. The control this row's `Closes when` asks for exists in it — baseline 4 is a same-size, same-wiring progressive net whose anterior column is *random* — and growth-with-content wins (transfer score 209 vs 134 on Pong variants, 132 vs 96 on Atari, 491 vs 185 on Labyrinth), with transfer still rising at four columns. Two things keep the row open. The schedule is not *the model's*: a column is committed at an externally supplied task boundary, one per task, so nothing here is the sparse per-module phase-offset vector the row asks for. And the twin is a frozen-random column rather than a trainable full-size network from step 0, so the comparison isolates the value of the stored content, not the value of the schedule. What the source adds beyond the result is the missing *observable*: Average Fisher Sensitivity spectra get sparser with every added column, on both the old features and the new column's own, which is a readable signal that the schedule has overshot — wired to nothing.
