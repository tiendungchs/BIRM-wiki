# G33 — Nothing decomposes a task into subgoals

**Status:** `PARTIAL`  
**Kind:** `part`  
**Level:** `L0`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/architectural-gaps.md]]  
**Closes when:** A configurator selects a subgoal sequence from the world model's own state, on a task whose decomposition was never demonstrated, and beats a flat planner given the same budget.

## Why it blocks the target

Hierarchical planning is now mechanised down to one missing step: a high-level action can be a *condition on the lower-level state* ([[wiki/entities/h-jepa.md]]), so subgoals need no symbolic vocabulary — but something must decide *which sequence of subgoals* a complex task becomes, and configure the cost module for each in turn. This is also the read half of G15 at a second level: not "when to plan" but "plan toward what"

## Best current answer

None. LeCun 2022 assigns the job to a **configurator** module, describes its interface precisely (modulate parameters and attention circuits of perception, world model and cost; extra input tokens in transformer modules), calls it "the most mysterious" part of the architecture, and leaves the decomposition itself "open for future investigation". Hierarchical reinforcement learning has been attacking the same question for longer and offers five partial answers, none settled: subgoals as innate/evolved primitives; states recurring in *rewarded* trajectories; **access points found by partitioning the estimated transition graph** (the only family that needs no signal the graph does not already contain); intrinsic reward for making unexpected salient events recur; and subgoals imported from an impasse or from watching another agent. The same source supplies the cost of getting it wrong — a wrong option set does not merely slow learning, it can make the optimal path unreachable (Botvinick, Niv & Barto 2009). **A sixth family is now built and measured** ([[wiki/entities/hisd.md]]): symbolise unlabelled observation trajectories by optimal-transport segmentation, compress the corpus with a grammar, and keep every rule the corpus uses twice — subgoals by *recurrence* rather than by bottleneck-ness, requiring no reward, no actions, no interaction and no transition graph, and producing the option-of-options nesting the options framework permits but never constructs. It is also the first evidence that the decomposition itself pays: with the discovered skill set held fixed, making the composite nodes selectable beats using the same skills flat, and both beat primitive-action PPO, which fails the task. What it does not supply is a *task-conditioned* decomposition — the grammar is induced once over a demonstration corpus, not configured per goal at query time (Harvey et al. 2026). **The graph-partitioning family is also built now, and it is the first decomposition in this row shown to beat its own flat baseline on path length** ([[wiki/entities/cscg.md]], George et al. 2021): InfoMap over a clone-structured hidden Markov model's learned transition matrix returns communities that never straddle the true rooms, a second pass returns the hyper-rooms, and top-down planning through the two levels gives paths 25% shorter than flat Dijkstra while size-matched surrogate communities that ignore room boundaries give paths 35% longer — so the benefit is attributable to the partition rather than to hierarchy as such. Its control adds a prerequisite this row did not state: the same partitioner on a successor representation over the *raw aliased observations* recovers nothing, so this family needs a de-aliaser upstream and not merely a transition estimate. Two things it still leaves to the experimenter: the number of levels is how many times the partitioner is re-run, and re-partitioning is never scheduled — a shortcut discovered after the decomposition was computed stays invisible to it. **The cost of leaving this to the experimenter is now visible on hardware** ([[wiki/entities/v-jepa-2.md]]): V-JEPA 2-AC solves real-robot pick-and-place from three hand-chosen goal images on a fixed 4/10/4 step timetable with no achievement detector, and this is exactly what reduces the planner to **horizon 1** — so the missing decomposition is not a convenience being substituted for, it is *search depth* being substituted for, and the source lists the un-decomposed version as a task it cannot do. It also shows the cheapest possible subgoal representation: not a symbol and not a learned cost but an *image*, whose embedding under the already-trained encoder is the thing descended toward

**The row has been missing an *objective*, and now has one** ([[wiki/concepts/optimal-hierarchy-criterion.md]], Solway et al. 2014). An option inventory is a model of the behaviours the agent must produce; its Bayesian model evidence over a task ensemble is provably equal, up to argmax, to both the shortest code length for those behaviours and the smallest geometric-mean number of trial-and-error attempts needed to discover the optimal policy of a randomly drawn task or subtask. The mechanism is that a good decomposition absorbs constraint into reusable option policies and leaves the *root* policy unconstrained wherever an option is running, and that multiplicity is what raises the marginal likelihood. The optimum lands on topological bottlenecks — four-rooms doorways, and for the Tower of Hanoi the three positions of the largest disk — which is the first normative licence for the graph-partitioning family this row already leans on, and it makes the row's seven families commensurable as description-length minimisers differing only in which stream they code. Four human experiments (`n` = 40, 10, 21, 35) show people find that parse with no prompt and from local adjacency alone (degree, exposure and salience equalised; bottleneck vertices chosen 4.4× chance rate) and plan through it region-first (faster correct RTs at boundary probes; equal-length Tower of Hanoi paths split by boundary-crossing count). **What it does not do is close the row**, and the reason is precise: the criterion's *data are the already-optimal flat policies for the whole ensemble*, so it scores a decomposition that presupposes the tasks are solved rather than selecting one from the world model's own state, which is exactly what `Closes when` demands. It also assumes the task ensemble is given, needs a deterministic reversible tabular graph, is only ever computed one level deep, returns ties on symmetric graphs, and is cheap to evaluate but expensive to maximise (search over all edge on/off partitions, run as binary integer programming under a genetic algorithm with up to 1000 restarts). The authors explicitly deny that humans compute it and nominate ordinary predictive learning ([[wiki/concepts/successor-representation.md]]) as the approximator — untested against their own four experiments

**An eighth family arrives and it is the first one that needs neither reward, task ensemble, nor an explicit graph** ([[wiki/concepts/eigenoption-discovery.md]], Machado, Bellemare & Bowling 2017). Diagonalise the state-transition Laplacian; each eigenvector `e` defines an intrinsic reward `rᵉ(s,s′) = eᵀ(φ(s′) − φ(s))` whose optimal policy is an option, with the initiation set `{s : ∃a, q > 0}` and termination set `{s : q ≤ 0 ∀a}` *derived* from the value function rather than designed, and termination guaranteed for `γ < 1` on finite `S`. Three things it supplies that no earlier row does: the graph never has to be built (SVD of an incidence matrix of feature differences gives the same eigenvectors, `TᵀT = 2L`), which carries the family into linear function approximation and onto Atari RAM features where an option scores 28 in Freeway having never seen the game's reward; the eigenvalue index is a **timescale ladder for free**, so the library is graded rather than uniform; and the same 64 options speed learning across different start/goal pairs, with swapping start and goal changing nothing. What it does not do is close the row: the option *count* is an explicit model-selection problem left to the experimenter, the eigenvector-ordering heuristic (smallest eigenvalues first) has no known justification under function approximation — the authors inspected all 1,024 Atari options by hand — the decomposition is one-shot with no incremental update as the graph estimate changes, and no hierarchy of options is built. It also sharpens the row's central assumption rather than confirming it: eigenoptions do **not** seek bottlenecks (in four-rooms the first doorway option is the fifth; room centres come first), and a doorway-only option set measurably *raises* diffusion time above a primitive-only random walk — so the bottleneck licence this row leans on may be scoped to the post-reward planning regime only (`T365`)

**And the row's null hypothesis, which it has never been run against** ([[wiki/concepts/hindsight-goal-relabelling.md]], Andrychowicz et al. 2017). HER decomposes nothing: it changes the *goal label* on transitions already collected, storing each one a second time under a goal the episode actually reached (`future` sampling: `k = 4`–`8` states drawn from the same episode after the transition). With that one line in the replay loop, a terminal binary reward `r(s,a,g) = −[f_g(s′) = 0]` trains DDPG to solve pushing, sliding and pick-and-place from **0**, and takes DQN on `n`-bit flipping from `n ≤ 13` to `n = 50` — the dense intermediate signal that subgoal decomposition is usually sold for, obtained with no option set, no bottleneck statistic, no graph and no extra environment interaction. Two things this fixes about the row. (i) Every discovery family here owes a comparison against a *relabelled flat* learner at matched interaction budget; none has run one, and `G33`'s only head-to-head evidence that decomposition pays ([[wiki/entities/hisd.md]], [[wiki/entities/cscg.md]]) is against unrelabelled flat baselines. (ii) It isolates what a decomposition must add beyond dense signal, because HER's own failure mode says what relabelling cannot do: `random` relabelling — goals drawn from the whole training history rather than from the trajectory's own future — is the one strategy that fails, so the mechanism works only where the manufactured goal is *reachable from the transition it is attached to*, and it supplies no ordering, no reuse across tasks and no goal outside the designer-chosen projection `m : S → G`


**The signal half of the row is now measured, and it sharpens what the missing half actually is** ([[wiki/concepts/pseudo-reward-prediction-error.md]], Ribas-Fernandes et al. 2011). A delivery task whose geometry holds distance-to-goal exactly constant along an ellipse while varying distance-to-subgoal produces a prediction error indexed to the *subgoal* — fronto-central negativity at Cz (EEG, `n` = 9), parametric dorsal anterior cingulate and bilateral anterior insula activation surviving a subgoal-displacement nuisance regressor (fMRI, `n` = 30), habenula and right amygdala at region-of-interest threshold, right nucleus accumbens for the positive-going case (`n` = 14) — with a behavioural control (`n` = 22) showing subgoal distance carries no weight in choice (`p` = 0.43) while goal distance carries a large one (`M = −7.6`), so the error is about a state the agent is not paid to reach. What this closes is `T136`; what it does for this row is subtract a worry rather than supply a mechanism. A self-set intermediate goal is *trainable by the same rule as a real one* — the error term exists and needs no new learning machinery — so every discovery family above can assume its output will be learnable once chosen. Three things it leaves exactly where they were: the subgoal is a visible task object placed by the experimenter and nothing selects it; the pseudo-reward's magnitude relative to primary reward is `1` : `1` by fiat, which is the farming question unasked; and the error appears in the same four structures as the ordinary reward prediction error, so the row inherits a signalling question it did not have (`T367` — one channel masked by the option identifier, or two)

**The ratio question now has an answer from the behavioural literature, and the answer is that it is not a hyperparameter** ([[wiki/concepts/conditioned-reinforcement.md]], Williams 1994). A stimulus paired with primary reward substitutes for it at a *measured* exchange rate set by its own pairing probability: a food-paired tone is worth **0.7** of a tone+food presentation when 50% of its presentations end in food, **0.4** at 30%, and **0** when it is never itself paired (Williams & Dunn 1991b, repeated-reversal discrimination learning). So the `r̃` : `r` ratio this row flagged as "set by fiat" is, in the animal, the intermediate target's empirical probability of leading to the primary reward — learned, not chosen, and revised downward by every unbacked delivery (`G125`). The same source supplies three further prices a decomposition pays that no criterion here charges for: value is fractional **delay reduction against the situation's average** and can be negative, so a subgoal's worth depends on the ambient reward rate with no relearning (Fantino 1969); the delay that sets it runs from the subgoal's **entry stimulus**, not from the action that selected it (Dunn, Williams & Royalty 1987); and inserting a single segmentation boundary into a fixed interval measurably *lowers* what the interval is worth, by an amount depending strongly on **where the boundary falls** — much worse early (2 s / 28 s of 30 s) than centrally — with the source stating plainly that no theory of conditioning explains the location dependence (Leung & Winton 1988). Against that, the row's caution: the response-rate advantage conferred by pairing vanishes at asymptote in Williams' own two experiments, so this literature prices *acquisition*, not steady-state performance

**A ninth family, and the one that makes the row's framing uncomfortable** ([[wiki/entities/option-critic.md]], Bacon, Harb & Precup 2017). Differentiate the discounted return with respect to the intra-option policy parameters `θ` and the termination parameters `ϑ`: both are policy-gradient theorems, so `π_ω`, `β_ω` and `π_Ω` are learned simultaneously from the environment's reward with **no subgoal, no pseudo-reward, no bottleneck statistic, no transition graph, no corpus and no task ensemble** — the modeller supplies only the option count. In four-rooms the termination probabilities concentrate near the doorways, so the bottleneck decomposition the rest of this row hunts for is recovered as a fixed point of return maximisation; the initial option set is learned at a rate *comparable to primitive actor–critic*, and it recovers faster than flat baselines when the goal is moved after 1000 episodes; in Pinball a near-optimal set is found by 40 episodes; in the Arcade Learning Environment, eight options on the DQN convolutional stack beat the original DQN in Asterix, Seaquest and Zaxxon within 200 episodes. Against the `Closes when` clause it is the closest thing in the row to a decomposition drawn from the agent's own state and beating a flat planner at matched budget — and it fails the clause on the word *subgoal*: nothing is ever selected as an intermediate target, so there is no decomposition to hand to a configurator, only a set of policies.

Three constraints it exports to the rest of the row. (i) **The return objective actively opposes hierarchy.** Trained on the return alone, the termination gradient drives `β → 1` and options collapse into primitives, because primitive actions suffice to solve any Markov decision process; the working system adds a hand-set `ξ = 0.01` to the advantage to stretch near-optimal options. So "nothing sets granularity" is not an omission across the families here but a consequence of scoring a decomposition on one task's return — the objective has to come from an ensemble ([[wiki/concepts/optimal-hierarchy-criterion.md]]), and `ξ` is that quantity paid as a scalar. (ii) **A decomposition learned from reward is a factorisation of that reward, not a model of the graph** — the doorway terminations appear because the goal is behind a doorway, so the transfer this family shows is transfer within one reward's neighbourhood and the row should not read it as bottleneck discovery. (iii) **The initiation set is the authors' own largest limitation**: `I_ω = S` throughout, and learning initiation sets under function approximation costs about as much as evaluating `π_Ω`, plus a flow condition linking initiation to termination to keep the augmented state–option chain ergodic — which is the first statement in this row of what it costs to make a decomposition *conditional on state*

**A tenth family, and the first whose subgoal is not a state** ([[wiki/entities/feudal-networks.md]], Vezhnevets et al. 2017). A Manager emits a *unit direction* `g_t` in a latent space `s = f^Mspace(z)` it learns for itself; a Worker is paid `r^I_t = (1/c) Σ_{i=1..c} d_cos(s_t − s_{t−i}, g_{t−i})` for making the latent state move that way, and receives the environment reward as well at gain `α`; the Manager is trained against the return by a **transition policy gradient** — the policy gradient of `p(s_{t+c}|s_t, μ(s_t,θ))` under a von Mises–Fisher model with mean direction `g_t` — so it learns from *where the Worker ends up* rather than from the Worker's trajectory, and no gradient crosses between the levels. On Montezuma's Revenge the emitted goals resolve into interpretable waypoints, two of which land where Kulkarni et al. hand-placed subgoals, and the agent clears room 1 in under 200 epochs against >300 for an LSTM baseline that then stagnates.

Four things it changes for this row. (i) **The subgoal vocabulary problem is dissolved differently from every family above**: a direction needs no graph, no bottleneck statistic, no corpus and no symbol set — only a latent space in which the lower level can reliably *cause* displacement, which the Worker's own intrinsic reward trains the space into being. It is also the first subgoal representation here that generalises by construction, since the same direction is a valid instruction over a large region of the space. (ii) **The `r̃` : `r` ratio gets its first machine measurement**: `α ~ U(0,1)` is swept, and the optimum is *domain-dependent* — high on Gravitar and Amidar, below 1 elsewhere — so the quantity this row flagged as "set by fiat" is not a constant, and nothing in the architecture derives it. (iii) **The temporal-extent premise is undercut by the source's own ablation**: setting the Manager's horizon `c = 1` hurts noticeably *less* than removing the second objective, the directional goals or the transition gradient, which says most of the benefit comes from training two levels under two objectives rather than from temporal abstraction. (iv) **The decomposition transfers across embodiment**: because the Manager's policy is stated in latent displacements, transferring a repeat-4 agent to repeat-1 needs only rescaled discounts, dilation and horizon, and beats every baseline on every environment.

Against `Closes when` it fails on the same word as option-critic — nothing is ever *selected* as an intermediate target, and the goals are re-emitted continuously with no termination condition, so there is no subgoal *sequence* to hand a configurator. It also inherits two unset integers (`c` and the dilated-LSTM radius `r`, both 10) in place of the granularity question, and it never nests: two levels only, with deeper hierarchies named as future work

**An eleventh family, and the first whose subgoal is a region rather than a point** ([[wiki/entities/diayn.md]], Eysenbach, Gupta, Ibarz & Levine 2018). Draw `z` from a **fixed uniform** prior, condition the policy on it, and pay `r_z(s,a) = log q_φ(z|s) − log p(z)` for being identifiable from the state visited; the library is the discriminator's decision regions, and the input is rollouts alone — no reward, no graph, no incidence matrix, no corpus, no demonstrator, no task ensemble.

Three things it settles or sharpens for this row. (i) **Bottleneck preference is derivable from an information objective with no graph.** On an `N×N` gridworld the unregularised optimum is any even partition; under entropy regularisation the only conflict between `H[A|S,Z]` and `−H[Z|S]` sits at *border* states, so the objective strictly prefers short borders and therefore cuts at bottlenecks, within `O(1/N)` of optimum. Three derivations — Bayesian model evidence ([[wiki/concepts/optimal-hierarchy-criterion.md]]), return gradient ([[wiki/entities/option-critic.md]]), and now skill–state mutual information — land on the doorway from incompatible premises. (ii) **Prior-collapse is the failure mode a discovery objective has to be engineered against, and the fix is not to learn.** Variational Intrinsic Control's learned `p(z)` cuts the effective skill count `e^{H[Z]}` by ~10×, because the diverse skills get sampled more and so get all the gradient; DIAYN's fixed prior prevents that at the price of making the skill count a hard commitment. (iii) **Diversity is not utility, measured.** The ant's learned skills all score *below* a do-nothing policy on the benchmark — an objective that maximises coverage of the reachable set has no term for whether the covered regions matter.

Against `Closes when` it fails on the same clause as option-critic and feudal networks and on one more: nothing is selected as an intermediate target (the index is drawn from a prior that serves nothing), there is no termination condition, and the skills are **frozen** before any task exists — so there is no subgoal *sequence*, and no mechanism to re-derive one when the task arrives.

**And the row's second null, which is the first thing to beat every family on it by three orders of magnitude** ([[wiki/entities/go-explore.md]], Ecoffet et al. 2021). Go-Explore decomposes nothing: no options, no levels, no pseudo-reward, no bottleneck statistic, no transition graph, no task ensemble, and *no intermediate target is ever selected*. It keeps an archive of visited cells, samples one by `W = 1/√(C_seen + 1)`, **returns to it without exploring on the way**, then takes uniform random actions for 100 steps. On the canonical instance of the problem this row exists to solve it reaches a mean above **1,700,000** on Montezuma's Revenge — above the human world record, 150× the state of the art, against ~2,600 for [[wiki/entities/feudal-networks.md]] — and takes Pitfall to 102,571 against a field-wide `0`. It also finds trajectories to all four shelves of a sparse-reward robot task where PPO sees zero reward in a billion frames.

Four things it changes for this row. (i) **The row's motivating premise is now contested** (`T402`): the diagnosis Go-Explore substitutes is *detachment* (a parametric agent overwrites the address of its own frontier) and *derailment* (a stochastic exploration policy cannot reproduce a long correct prefix), both of which are consequences of storing the frontier in weights and neither of which is a decomposition failure. (ii) **The isolating control is run and it is decisive about the mechanism**: count-based intrinsic motivation with the *identical* domain-knowledge cell representation scores 12,240 against 1.7M, and finds zero reward on the robotics task. Representation and novelty statistic held fixed, the only difference is that the frontier is a sampling weight over an external store rather than a gradient into a policy — so the row's families owe a comparison against *archive-and-return*, not merely against flat PPO, and the quantity to hold fixed in that comparison is the state abstraction. (iii) **The abstraction slot is the same one every family here leaves empty, seen from a third angle**: the state→cell map `φ` is designer-given (downscaled greyscale frame, or hand-authored room/`x`/`y`/keys, worth 150× in score), which is [[wiki/concepts/hindsight-goal-relabelling.md]]'s `m : S → G` and [[wiki/concepts/node-definition-problem.md]]'s vertex set again. Go-Explore adds the one mitigation the others lack — **the granularity is re-optimised during the run**. (iv) **Its own cell-selection weight prefers frontier, not bottleneck**: `W_location = (2 − h)/10 + k` rewards a cell for *lacking* archived horizontal neighbours, which is a third datum for `T365`.

Against `Closes when` it fails on the entire clause — nothing is selected, configured or sequenced — and its headline numbers assume a **restorable simulator**. Removing that assumption (policy-based Go-Explore) costs 1.7M → 97,728 and, tellingly, reintroduces a subgoal sequence: the goal-conditioned policy is fed successive cells from the archived trajectory under a soft window `N_w = 10`, with intermediate reward `1` and terminal reward `3`. So the version of Go-Explore that runs without an exemption *does* decompose — it just reads the decomposition out of a stored trajectory instead of discovering one, which is the narrowest live form of this row's question
