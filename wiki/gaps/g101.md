# G101 — Nothing in the wiki scales *edge density* rather than unit count, and no result says what a higher synapse-per-unit ratio buys

**Status:** `OPEN`  
**Kind:** `part`  
**Level:** `L3`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/architectural-gaps.md]]  
**Closes when:** An experiment scales edge density at fixed unit count and reports what the higher synapse-per-unit ratio buys, separating it from the effect of adding units.

## Why it blocks the target

Human cerebral cortex is **2.75×** chimpanzee volume with only **1.25×** the neurons (Shariff 1953): the tripling that supposedly explains human cognition is overwhelmingly neuropil — dendrites, axons, synapses — not units. Corroborating measurements point the same way: the glia:neuron ratio is the highest of any primate and rises with brain size; the excess temporal-lobe white matter sits in **gyral** rather than core white matter, i.e. it is *local* connectivity between neighbouring fields; humans have as much eulaminate cortex as predicted and slightly **fewer** eulaminate neurons. Every scaling axis the wiki holds is a unit-count or depth axis, and the one biological example of a scale-up that produced a qualitative change is not on that axis. It blocks the target because the wiki's `G70`/`G21` failures are attributed to missing mechanisms and the comparative record says the same failures might be a connectivity-density regime the wiki has never entered. **The row's premise is now contested, and the challenge also supplies the sweep the row asks for** (Herculano-Houzel 2009, [[wiki/concepts/cellular-scaling-rules.md]]): whole-brain isotropic-fractionator counts across 18 species give the edge-density axis a measured exponent, `M ∼ N^a` with `a > 1` meaning average unit size — soma plus entire arbour — grows with unit count. Rodents scale edge density (`N^1.550` whole brain, `N^1.744` in cortex, falling neuronal density, rising glia:neuron ratio); primates do not (`N^1.056`, `N^1.077`, invariant density and invariant non-neuronal ratio). So nature *has* run this row's experiment across two orders, and the clade that bought units at constant wiring-per-unit holds **seven times** the neurons of the clade that bought wiring-per-unit, at the same 1.5 kg. Under the primate exponent, Shariff's 2.75×-volume-for-1.25×-neurons is ~2.5× the neurons instead ([[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]] T301), and the human cortex's 16 billion neurons are what a primate regression fitted without humans predicts. Two things keep the row open anyway: the primate cortical fit contains **no great apes**, so a hominid-specific departure is untested, and the comparison is between-clade rather than at fixed unit count, which is what the `Closes when` actually asks for

## Best current answer

**Nothing measured.** The wiki has the ingredients and no experiment: [[wiki/concepts/small-world-topology.md]] holds the wiring-cost statistics, [[wiki/concepts/dendritic-computation.md]] holds the per-unit-compute argument (a single neuron with active dendrites is a multilayer network), and [[wiki/concepts/emergent-modularity.md]] holds the one causal proposal that lives on this axis — that generalised systematicity is bought by afferent *diversity* into a fixed composition site, which is a fan-in claim and is directly sweepable. The source does not say what the ratio buys either: capacity, precision, mixing, or nothing beyond the metabolic price of being large. `G101` and `G100` are the two halves of the same complaint — the wiki scales the wrong variable and does so all at once

**The converse sweep now exists on the machine side, and it does not close the row.** Golubeva et al. 2021 hold the *weight* count fixed and raise unit count by static random sparsification ([[wiki/concepts/width-versus-parameter-count.md]]): ResNet-18 at 11.7M weights reaches 70.66 top-1 at width 181 against 70.91 for the dense 90.7M model, and the optimum for convolutional models sits at **1–10% connectivity** (widening factor 3–10), with the turnover caused by the optimiser failing to fit the training set rather than by lost capacity. That prices *units at fixed edges* and says the fixed-budget answer is to spend on units — the mirror image of what this row asks, which is edges at fixed units. What it does supply is a warning about the row's own arithmetic: unit count and weight count come apart by up to 7.8× at matched accuracy, so "higher synapse-per-unit ratio" must be swept with unit count pinned or it will re-measure width.

**The per-neuron side now has measurements. They are correlational and second-hand, so the row stays open** (Galakhova et al. 2022, [[wiki/entities/human-supragranular-pyramidal-neuron.md]]).

| What varies | Unit count | What it tracks | Source (via the review) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Human vs mouse L2/3: ~2× synapses, ~3× dendrite, at roughly constant synapse density | Not held | Species | DeFelipe et al. 2002; Mohan et al. 2015; Elston et al. 2001; Sherwood et al. 2020 |
| Human individuals: thicker L2/L3 made of **larger cells at lower density, not more neurons** | **Roughly unchanged** | Higher general and verbal IQ | Heyer et al. 2021 |
| Human individuals: larger, more complex L2/L3 dendrites | — | Higher IQ; models on the measured dendrites give higher temporal precision for fast inputs | Goriounova et al. 2018 |

The Heyer row is the nearest thing to this row's experiment: wiring per unit varies while unit number does not, and a behavioural score moves with it. It does not meet `Closes when` for three reasons: nothing was manipulated; cell size, dendrite length and synapse count vary together, so edges are not separated from per-unit nonlinearity; and the "buys" column is an IQ score plus a modelled precision gain, not a capacity or accuracy measurement. The review adds one candidate answer to what the ratio buys — a per-branch exclusive-or nonlinearity and ~1000 Hz input tracking — both on the per-unit-compute side, not the edge-count side.

**The machine side now has a two-point sweep at fixed unit count. It does not price the ratio, so the row stays open** (Mocanu et al. 2018, [[wiki/entities/sparse-evolutionary-training.md]]). SET keeps layer widths fixed and sets edges per unit through `ε`: `n^W = ε(n^k + n^{k−1})`, i.e. ~11 (RBM) or ~20 (MLP, CNN) edges per unit against the dense layer's `n^{k−1}`.

| Density at fixed width | Result |
|---|---|
| Dense vs SET, RBM (11 datasets) | Sparse better on 7/11; MNIST −86.41 nats at 2% of the weights |
| Dense vs SET, MLP (CIFAR-10) | Sparse better, at ~1% of the weights (74.84%) |
| Dense vs FixProb vs SET, CNN top layers (CIFAR-10) | 87.48 / 88.26 / **90.02**%, at 8.4M / 185k / 185k weights |

Read against `Closes when`: the axis is right (edges at fixed units), but three things are missing. (1) **Only two densities**: `ε` was set once, by random search on MNIST, and no intermediate point is reported. (2) **The dense end overfits**: the authors attribute the stability of both sparse variants to having few parameters, so "denser is not better" mixes the value of edges with a regularisation penalty. (3) **i.i.d. accuracy only**, so the row's question of what the ratio buys for `G70`/`G21`-type failures is untouched. What it does settle: at fixed units and a fixed low ratio, *where* the edges are is worth +1.8 points over a random placement on the one CNN tested. So a ratio sweep has to fix the topology rule, or it measures placement along with density.
