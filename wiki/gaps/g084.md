# G84 — No architecture in the wiki knows the topological position of its own modules, and none has a component whose job is to be the crossing point

**Status:** `OPEN`  
**Kind:** `arrangement`  
**Registry:** [[wiki/architectural-gaps.md]]  
**Closes when:** An architecture knows its own modules' topological positions and has a component whose function is to be the crossing point, with the sparse-core arrangement shown to beat all-to-all at matched parameter count.

## Why it blocks the target

Cortex does not wire its modules all-to-all: the graph is 3% dense, 54% of the relational mass stays inside a unit, and a small bilateral posterior medial/parietal core carries **>70% of all between-module edge mass** while being simultaneously the highest in degree, strength, betweenness, efficiency and erosion resistance (Hagmann et al. 2008). Two things follow that no wiki architecture supplies. First, the *diagnostic*: the participation index `P_i = 1 − Σ_s (κ_is/k_i)²` separates a unit that aggregates within its own specialisation (provincial hub) from one that is the only path between specialisations (connector hub), and no model here reports it — so which of its parts is load-bearing for cross-module transfer is unknown, and its capacity is therefore unbudgeted. Second, the *cost*: centrality predicts regional metabolic demand at `r² = 0.49`, meaning a hub is paid for continuously whether or not it is used, whereas every routing mechanism in the wiki (attention, a mixture-of-experts router, hand-specified inter-module wiring) is treated as free. The gap is not "add a router" — it is that placement of the integrative bottleneck is currently made implicitly and never measured

## Best current answer

Nothing directly. The nearest things are *functional* rather than topological: [[wiki/entities/pbwm.md]] and [[wiki/entities/h-jepa.md]] specify inter-module wiring by hand, and [[wiki/concepts/attention.md]] computes a routing weight per query without any notion of a persistent crossing point. The biological answer is a **club, not a star** — degree is exponentially and not power-law distributed (~10-fold range), connector hubs sit without exception on one anatomical axis, and they are bilaterally duplicated, so the design is redundancy across a small set of comparable mutually-connected hubs. The measures needed are cheap and task-free (k-core erosion needs no weights, no loss and no forward pass), which also makes this the wiki's only candidate importance signal that a continual learner could compute *before* seeing the next task ([[wiki/concepts/continual-learning.md]]). **And the row now has a hardware constraint from the one family that actually distributes modules across devices** ([[wiki/entities/sparsely-gated-moe.md]]): a conditionally-computed module's ratio of compute to input/output traffic equals its hidden-layer width, and that ratio must exceed the cluster's compute-to-bandwidth ratio ("thousands to one") for the module to be worth addressing at all. So there is a **floor under module size** and, equivalently, a price on every between-module edge — the machine analogue of the wiring cost that produces cortex's 3%-dense graph, and the reason the wiki's fine-grained module proposals have no efficient implementation on current hardware independently of whether they would learn. **And the row now has a scaling law rather than a fixed price** (Ardesch et al. 2022, [[wiki/concepts/connectivity-scaling-bottleneck.md]]): across 14 primates spanning 350× in cerebral volume, the crossing point does not merely cost something — it scales *sub*-isometrically on the thing it must serve (corpus callosum cross-section `∼ cortical surface^0.88`, from 1 cm² of callosum per 90 cm² of cortex in the galago to 1 per 211 cm² in humans), and every downstream consequence follows: density 52% → 31–37%, the long-connection fraction 63–71% → 40%, path length and clustering both rising with size at matched density (β = +0.74, +0.75). So the correct machine target is not "add a crossing point" but "budget its capacity as a sub-linear function of module count and expect local clustering to rise to compensate" — and the arrangement's benefit must be demonstrated **at several sizes**, because a sparse core beating all-to-all is a claim whose sign can change with `N`

**And the row now has a rival that removes the component entirely** (Seguin, Sporns & Zalesky 2023, [[wiki/concepts/network-communication-models.md]]). Two things follow. First, a *caveat on the row's own evidence*: betweenness centrality — one of the six converging measures that located the core — counts shortest paths, so it presupposes a routing protocol requiring every node to hold a map of the whole graph, which the review argues a decentralised system cannot implement. The core is a bottleneck *under centralised routing*; its load under communicability or greedy navigation is unmeasured ([[wiki/empirical-tensions.md]] T316), and only a small proportion of connections lie on any shortest path at all, so a betweenness account of traffic assigns zero load to most of the wire. Second, the *alternative design*: **greedy navigation** forwards each signal to whichever neighbour is closest to the target under a node distance metric, using only local knowledge, and achieves **70–100% of shortest-path signalling efficiency** in human, mouse and macaque connectomes (Seguin et al. 2018). If the metric reflects connection probability — i.e. if it is a correctly learned embedding — the routing information lives in the *embedding* rather than in any node, and the crossing point stops being a component that must be built and budgeted. The cost accounting also changes: this is `O(degree)` per hop against the `O(E)` global scoring of a mixture-of-experts gate, which is the informational-cost dimension the hardware argument above does not contain
