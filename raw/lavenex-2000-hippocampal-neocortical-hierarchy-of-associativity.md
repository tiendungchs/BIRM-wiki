**ABSTRACT:** **The structures forming the medial temporal lobe appear to**
**be** **necessary** **for** **the** **establishment** **of** **long-term** **declarative** **memory.** **In**
**particular, they may be involved in the “consolidation” of information in**
**higher-order** **associational** **cortices,** **perhaps** **through** **feedback** **projec-**
**tions.** **This** **review** **highlights** **the** **fact** **that** **the** **medial** **temporal** **lobe** **is**
**organized** **as** **a** **hierarchy** **of** **associational** **networks.** **Indeed,** **associational**
**connections** **within** **the** **perirhinal,** **parahippocampal,** **and** **entorhinal** **cor-**
**tices** **enables** **a** **significant** **amount** **of** **integration** **of** **unimodal** **and** **poly-**
**modal** **inputs,** **so** **that** **only** **highly** **integrated** **information** **reaches** **the**
**remainder** **of** **the** **hippocampal** **formation.** **The** **feedback** **efferent** **projec-**
**tions** **from** **the** **perirhinal** **and** **parahippocampal** **cortices** **to** **the** **neocortex**
**largely** **reciprocate** **the** **afferent** **projections** **from** **the** **neocortex** **to** **these**
**areas.** **There** **are,** **however,** **noticeable** **differences** **in** **the** **degree** **of** **reci-**
**procity of connections between the perirhinal and parahippocampal cor-**
**tices** **and** **certain** **areas** **of** **the** **neocortex,** **in** **particular** **in** **the** **frontal** **and**
**temporal lobes. These observations are particularly important for models**
**of hippocampal-neocortical interaction and long-term storage of informa-**
**tion in the neocortex. Furthermore, recent functional studies suggest that**
**the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices are more than interfaces for**
**communication** **between** **the** **neocortex** **and** **the** **hippocampal** **formation.**
**These structures participate actively in memory processes, but the precise**
**role** **they** **play** **in** **the** **service** **of** **memory** **or** **other** **cognitive** **functions** **is**

**INTRODUCTION:** **MEDIAL** **TEMPORAL** **LOBE**

**AND** **MEMORY**

Beginning with the description of the amnesic patient H.M. by Scoville
and Milner (1957), a variety of lines of evidence have clearly established the
importance of the medial temporal lobe to memory function. Recent advances in characterizing human memory processes indicate that the brain
supports multiple memory systems, and that these distinct memory systems
are subserved by different neural systems (Squire, 1992). In humans, damage
to the medial temporal lobe results in a severe memory impairment that is

characterized by rapid forgetting of declarative information but leaves intact a rather disparate set of memories
grouped under the term “nondeclarative memory,” including procedural learning, priming, nonassociative
learning, and simple classical conditioning (Squire and
Zola-Morgan, 1996).

The structures forming the medial temporal lobe, including the dentate gyrus, hippocampus, subiculum, presubiculum, parasubiculum, and entorhinal, perirhinal,
and parahippocampal cortices (Fig. 1), appear to be necessary for the establishment of long-term declarative
memory in human and nonhuman mammals (Squire and
Zola-Morgan, 1996). The structures of the medial temporal lobe are thought to be involved in the “consolidation” of information in higher-order associational cortices via feedback projections to these cortical areas, thus
enabling them to serve as the final repositories for longterm memories (Squire and Alvarez, 1995). Consistent
with this hypothesis, temporally graded retrograde amnesia has been demonstrated both in humans and animals
after selective lesions of the medial temporal lobe (Scoville and Milner, 1957; Cho et al., 1995; Myhrer and
Wangen, 1996). This model suggests a temporary role
for the medial temporal lobe circuitry in the establishment of long-term memory in the neocortex (Squire,
1992). In this model, information initially processed in
the neocortex is transmitted to the hippocampal complex
through the different structures of the medial temporal
lobe, i.e., the entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices. This information is further processed within
the remainder of the hippocampal formation, and the
resultant “representation” is returned to the regions of
origin, enabling the consolidation of long-term memory
in the neocortex. The hippocampal circuitry may contribute to the formation of new relationships in longterm memory, such as those formed when associating a
stimulus and its spatial/temporal context (thus representing a new event or episode), or when associating facts and
the semantic context to which they belong (thus representing a new concept). This model, however, does not
delineate more specifically the possible roles of the different structures of the medial temporal lobe in information
processing.

The development of animal models has been invaluable to our
understanding of the function of the medial temporal lobe in
memory processes. Animal studies have begun to contribute important information on the precise roles of each of the medial
temporal lobe regions in the process of normal memory function.
Studies carried out mainly in the monkey and the rat show that
lesions restricted to the perirhinal or the parahippocampal cortices,
for instance, produce a memory deficit as severe as lesions restricted
to the hippocampus (Zola-Morgan et al., 1989; Otto and Eichenbaum, 1992; Meunier et al., 1993; Mumby and Pinel, 1994) and
can also exacerbate the deficit observed after lesion of the hippocampus (Zola-Morgan et al., 1993; Wiig and Bilkey, 1995).
Similarly, animal models have shown that the role of the entorhinal
cortex is distinct from that of the hippocampus proper (Schenk
and Morris, 1985; Meunier et al., 1993; Leonard et al., 1995).
Recently, electrophysiological studies have also demonstrated the
importance of the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices in
memory consolidation processes in animal models. In rats, perirhinal cortex activation appears to promote enhancement in corticocortical pathways secondarily activated by perirhinal inputs
(Ivanco et al., 1996). In monkeys, Higuchi and Miyashita (1996)
demonstrated that lesions of the entorhinal and perirhinal cortices
prevented the formation of mnemonic neuronal responses to visual
paired associates in the inferotemporal cortex.

It is now clear that these extrahippocampal structures do not
simply funnel information from neocortical or subcortical areas to
the hippocampus, but rather participate actively in memory processing. Unfortunately, however, little is known about the specific
processing that takes place at each stage along the neocorticalhippocampal loop. We briefly describe the connectivity of these
structures, in particular the connections between the neocortex,
perirhinal, parahippocampal, and entorhinal cortices, as well as the
important networks of intrinsic, associational connections within
these structures.

Our presentation will be organized around several critical questions regarding the neuroanatomy of the medial temporal memory
system:

1. What cortical regions send information to the hippocampal
formation?
2. What are the neuroanatomical features that characterize the
structures forming the neocortical-hippocampal loop?
3. How are the local connections organized in each of these regions and what are the implications for the types of information
that ultimately reaches the hippocampal formation?
4. To what extent are the projections to the medial temporal lobe
reciprocated?

**NEUROANATOMY** **OF** **THE** **ENTORHINAL,**

**PERIRHINAL,** **AND** **PARAHIPPOCAMPAL**

**CORTICES**

The entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices surround the rhinal sulcus on the ventromedial surface of the monkey

brain (Figs. 1, 2). The terminology for the various subdivisions of
these regions of cortex has been described previously (Amaral et al.,
1987; Insausti et al., 1987). Briefly, the entorhinal cortex is subdivided into six parts that are arranged from rostral to caudal. Rostrally and medially is the olfactory division (EO), so named because
it is the only entorhinal field that receives a direct projection from
the olfactory bulb. EO is bordered laterally by the rostral subdivision (ER) of the entorhinal cortex. Area ER, in turn, is bordered
laterally by the lateral subdivision of the entorhinal cortex (EL).
Both EO and ER are bordered caudally by the intermediate subdivision of the entorhinal cortex (EI). Finally the caudal portion of
the entorhinal cortex is made up of the larger caudal division (EC),
and a smaller caudal limiting division (ECL).

The perirhinal cortex consists of a smaller medially situated area
35 and a larger laterally situated area 36. For most of its rostrocaudal extent, area 35 is confined to the fundus and lateral bank of the
rhinal sulcus. Area 36 is located just lateral to area 35. At the most
rostral and dorsal extent of the perirhinal cortex is area 36d, which
comprises approximately the dorsal one fourth of what is typically
referred to as temporal pole. Caudally adjacent to area 36d is area
36r (further subdivided into 36rm and 36rl). It is bounded laterally
by the unimodal visual area TE. The caudal extreme of the perirhinal cortex has been called area 36c (further subdivided into 36cm
and 36cl). Areas 36cm and 36cl are medially adjacent to the intermediate and caudal divisions of the entorhinal cortex (areas EI and

EC), and are typically bounded laterally by the most rostral portion
of area TF of the parahippocampal cortex.

The parahippocampal cortex is caudally adjacent to the perirhinal cortex and consists of a smaller, medially situated area TH and
a larger laterally situated area TF (Von Bonin and Bailey, 1947),
which is comprised of two subdivisions, a medially located area
TFm and a laterally adjacent area TFl (Suzuki and Amaral, 1994a;
Kobayashi and Amaral, 1999).

**Connectivity**

Neuroanatomical studies have provided detailed information
about the interconnections between the structures of the medial
temporal lobe and other cortical areas (Figs. 2, 3). A prominent
feature of the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices is their connectivity with the hippocampal formation, particularly the entorhinal cortex. In the monkey, these cortical regions provide almost
two thirds of the neocortical input to the entorhinal cortex (Insausti et al., 1987), which in turn provides the predominant cortical
input to the dentate gyrus and hippocampus via the perforant path
(Amaral et al., 1987, Van Hoesen and Pandya, 1975; Witter et al.,
1989; Suzuki and Amaral, 1990; Witter and Amaral, 1991). The
functional significance of the perirhinal and parahippocampal projections to the entorhinal cortex is tied to the fact that the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices are themselves the focus of converging inputs from unimodal and polymodal associational
cortices (Jones and Powell, 1970; Van Hoesen and Pandya, 1975,
Van Hoesen et al., 1975, Suzuki and Amaral, 1994a). The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices are also in receipt of efferent
projections from the entorhinal cortex that generally reciprocate
the input pathways (Suzuki and Amaral, 1994b), and in turn

project back to the neocortex (Van Hoesen, 1982; Lavenex et al.,

Another important and hitherto underappreciated feature of
these cortices is the important network of intrinsic associational
connections at every level of the neocortical-hippocampal loop.
The intrinsic connections within the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices potentially lead to substantial integration of
information that reaches different rostrocaudal and mediolateral
regions of these cortices. Moreover, the connections between the
perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices provide another level of
associativity and integration of information. Information processed within these cortices is then transmitted to the entorhinal
cortex, the second stage of the neocortical-hippocampal loop that
represents a hierarchically higher level of associativity, where the
important network of intrinsic connections further increases the
level of integration of information before it is transmitted to the
hippocampus. The remaining part of the hippocampal formation,
with its several associational networks within the dentate gyrus,
CA3, and subiculum, constitutes the ultimate level of integration
of information.

It is this hierarchy of associativity that most uniquely characterizes the neuroanatomy of the medial temporal lobe. We will provide a brief overview of these connections and then raise some
possible implications of this type of convergent/divergent pattern
of connections.

**Cortical** **Afferent** **Projections** **to** **the** **Perirhinal**
**and** **Parahippocampal** **Cortices**

The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices receive convergent
inputs from a variety of unimodal and polymodal association cortices in the temporal, frontal, and parietal lobes (Fig. 2). Unimodal
associational inputs arise from higher-order somatosensory, auditory, and visual association cortices (Van Hoesen and Pandya,
1975; Van Hoesen et al., 1975; Tranel et al., 1988; Suzuki and
Amaral, 1994a). While the majority of the cortical inputs to the
perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices are from visual areas, different types of visual information reach each of these cortices.
Visual object information from area TE reaches predominantly the
perirhinal cortex, whereas visuospatial information from area V4
and the posterior parietal cortex is directed more specifically to the
parahippocampal cortex. Cortices associated with somatosensory
processing project to both the perirhinal and parahippocampal
cortices, and the auditory association cortex projects mainly to the
parahippocampal cortex (Tranel et al., 1988; Suzuki and Amaral,
1994a). The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices receive
polysensory information from the ventrolateral and orbitofrontal
cortices, cingulate and retrosplenial cortices, posterior parietal cortex, and polymodal region of the dorsal bank of the superior temporal sulcus (Suzuki and Amaral, 1994a).

**Intrinsic** **Connections** **Within** **the** **Perirhinal** **and**
**Parahippocampal** **Cortices**

Neuroanatomical tracer studies have revealed a high level of
associational connections within the perirhinal and parahip

pocampal cortices (Suzuki and Amaral, 1994b; Lavenex and Amaral, unpublished observations).

Areas 36r and 36c of the perirhinal cortex are highly and reciprocally interconnected, whereas the most dorsal part of the perirhinal cortex, area 36d, seems to have fewer associational connections
with the rest of the perirhinal cortex. The most rostral and medial
part of area 36r of the perirhinal cortex projects heavily to itself,
areas 35 and 36c, but has practically no projections directed to area
36d. Interestingly, the more lateral aspects of area 36r send heavy
projections to areas 35, 36r, and 36c, as well as light projections to
area 36d. The caudal area of the perirhinal cortex, area 36c,
projects heavily to itself and moderately to heavily to area 36r; there
are practically no projections from area 36c to area 36d.

Areas TF and TH are strongly and reciprocally interconnected.
There does not seem to be any rostrocaudal organization of the
projections, as the most rostral and caudal parts of area TF project
heavily to the entire rostrocaudal axis of the parahippocampal cortex (area TF and area TH). In contrast, there seems to be some
segregation of the projections along the mediolateral axis. Indeed,
area TH projects heavily to itself and the medial portion of area TF,
whereas the projections to the lateral portion of area TF are only
moderate. Similarly, the lateral area seems to project heavily to
itself, moderately to medial area TF, and only lightly to area TH.
Projections originating at the border between the medial and lateral TF project strongly to all mediolateral portions of TF and only
moderately to area TH.

These networks of intrinsic associational connections within the
perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices potentially lead to the
substantial integration of information that reaches different rostrocaudal and mediolateral regions of these cortices.

**Reciprocal** **Connections** **Between** **the** **Perirhinal**
**and** **Parahippocampal** **Cortices**

As for the intrinsic connections within the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices, neuroanatomical tracer studies have revealed an important network of reciprocal, associational connections between the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices (Suzuki
and Amaral, 1994b; Lavenex and Amaral, unpublished observations).

As stated previously, a prominent input to the perirhinal cortex
arises from the parahippocampal cortex. Retrograde tracer injection studies show that areas 35, 36r, and 36c of the perirhinal

of areas TF and TH, whereas more caudal portions of areas TF and
TH project only lightly to areas 35, 36r, and 36c of the perirhinal
cortex. In contrast, the most dorsal part of the perirhinal cortex,
area 36d, receives a prominent projection from the entire rostrocaudal extent of the parahippocampal cortex. Anterograde tracer

the perirhinal cortex, areas 36d and 36c, whereas the projections to
areas 35 and 36r are rather sparse. The mid-rostrocaudal region of
area TF projects moderately to heavily to area 36d, lightly to moderately to area 36c, and sparsely to areas 35 and 36r. The caudal

**Cortical** **Afferent** **Projections** **to** **the** **Entorhinal**
**Cortex**

The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices, taken together,
constitute the major sources of cortical input to the monkey entorhinal cortex, the second stage of the neocortical-hippocampal
loop. Their patterns of connection differ significantly, however,
with the perirhinal cortex projections terminating in the rostral
two thirds of the entorhinal cortex, and the parahippocampal cortex projections terminating in the caudal two thirds (Suzuki and
Amaral, 1994b). It is important to note, however, that this segregation of projections does not necessarily reflect a segregation of
sensory inputs within the entorhinal cortex. Visuospatial information from area V4 and the posterior parietal cortex, for instance,
can potentially reach every level of the entorhinal cortex, either
through a disynaptic connection via the parahippocampal cortex
or a trisynaptic connection via projections to the parahippocampal
cortex relayed through the perirhinal cortex. The perirhinal and
parahippocampal cortices are not the only cortical regions that
project to the entorhinal cortex (Fig. 2). It also receives prominent
projections from the superior temporal gyrus, the insular cortex,
the orbitofrontal cortex, the cingulate cortex, and the retrosplenial
cortex (Insausti et al., 1987). As with the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices, each of these cortical regions receives rather
diverse neocortical inputs, and each of these regions can be classified as “polysensory associational cortices” (Pandya and Yeterian,
1985). Thus, while the hippocampal formation appears to receive
direct inputs from a relatively limited number of neocortical regions, each of these regions is a convergence zone that likely provides information from a much broader extent of cortex. In this
way, the hippocampal formation is ultimately linked to much of
the processing that takes place within the neocortex.

**Intrinsic** **Connections** **Within** **the** **Entorhinal**
**Cortex**

The entorhinal cortex constitutes the second stage of the neocortical-hippocampal loop that represents a hierarchically higher
level of associativity than the polysensory cortices that provide its
input. Within the entorhinal cortex a major network of intrinsic,
associational connections further increases the level of integration
of information before it is transmitted to the hippocampus. The
dominant organizational feature of intrinsic connections within
the entorhinal cortex is the relative segregation of projections into
three rostrocaudally oriented bands (Chrobak and Amaral, 1997):
a lateral band that encompasses field EL and the most lateral aspect
of fields EC and ECL; a wider, intermediate band that subsumes
much of fields ER, EI, EC, and ECL; and a rostromedial band that
includes EO, and the most medial aspect of EI. These data confirm
earlier work carried out in the rat that entorhinal neurons are
organized into three separate associational networks that may serve
as a substrate for parallel processing within the hippocampal formation (Dolorfo and Amaral, 1998). A comparison with previously described connectivity of the rat entorhinal cortex, however,
indicates clear differences between species. In the rodent entorhinal cortex, the distribution of projections from any location extends the entire rostrocaudal extent of the entorhinal cortex. This is
not the case in the monkey, where the distribution of projections
from any location extends about half the rostrocaudal extent of the
entorhinal cortex. Thus, there appears to be greater segregation of
connectivity domains within the entorhinal cortex and with respect to its interconnections with the hippocampus in the monkey
as compared to the rat. This higher degree of segregation might
indicate a higher level of parallel processing in the entorhinal cortex
of the monkey than in the rat.

The associational connections in the entorhinal cortex originate
both in the deep layers (V and VI) and in layer II. The rostrocaudal
organization and the total extent of projections within both the
deep and superficial layers were unrelated to whether cells originating these projections were located in the deep or superficial layers
(Chrobak and Amaral, unpublished observations). However,
whereas the deep cells projected both to other deep cells as well as
to superficial layers, the layer II associational connection was maintained primarily within layer II. Thus, the layer II cells which
generate the perforant path projection to the dentate gyrus and
hippocampus are themselves interconnected by a dense network of
associational connections. Moreover, the associational connections from deep to superficial layers have the potential of closing
the hippocampal loop, since the output of the hippocampus and
subiculum to the entorhinal cortex terminates mainly in the deep
layers.

hierarchy of associativity along the neocortical-hippocampal loop.

The entorhinal cortex projects via the perforant path to the
dentate gyrus, hippocampus, and subiculum. The projections to
the dentate gyrus and CA3 field of the hippocampus originate
mainly from cells in layer II. The projections to CA1 and subiculum, in contrast, originate mainly from cells in layer III. The dentate gyrus gives rise to only one major connection within the hippocampal formation. The axons of the granule cells, the mossy
fibers, project to the CA3 region and also send collaterals which
terminate on cells within the polymorphic layer of the dentate
gyrus. The cells of the polymorphic layer, and particularly one cell
type called the mossy cell, give rise to extensive associational connections that terminate on the proximal dendrites of granule cells
throughout much of the length of the dentate gyrus. Projections
arising from the CA3 pyramidal cells include collaterals to other
CA3 pyramidal cells that constitute an extensive system of associational connections, as well as the Schaffer collaterals that constitute
the major projection to the CA1 pyramidal cells. Both the CA3CA3 associational connections and the Schaffer collaterals diverge
widely from their point of origin. A single CA3 pyramidal cell may
contact CA1 cells throughout 75% of the length of the hippocampus. The CA1 field of the hippocampus projects both to the subiculum and to the deep layers of the entorhinal cortex. Unlike the
CA3 field, however, there are very few associational connections
within CA1. This fact distinguishes CA1 from most other regions
of the medial temporal lobe.

**Efferent** **Projections** **From** **the** **Entorhinal** **Cortex**
**to** **Perirhinal** **and** **Parahippocampal** **Cortices**

The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices receive feedback
efferent projections from the hippocampus and the entorhinal cortex that largely reciprocate the input pathways (Van Hoesen, 1982;
Suzuki and Amaral, 1994b). Accordingly, the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices play a unique role in the dissemination of
hippocampal output. The degree of reciprocity of the interconnections of the entorhinal cortex with the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices differs slightly. The parahippocampal/entorhinal connections have a high degree of reciprocity, so that the caudal
entorhinal mainly projects to the parahippocampal cortex. In contrast, the degree of reciprocity of the perirhinal/entorhinal interconnections varies depending on the mediolateral position within
the perirhinal cortex; medial portions of the perirhinal cortex exhibit a higher degree of reciprocity than the lateral portion. Overall, however, one can consider the efferent projections from the
entorhinal cortex to the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices as
feedback projections that are largely reciprocal.

**Efferent** **Projections** **From** **Perirhinal** **and**
**Parahippocampal** **Cortices** **to** **the** **Neocortex**

The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices do not simply relay information from the neocortex to the hippocampus, but receive strong efferent projections from the hippocampal formation
(see above), and in turn project back to the neocortex. The efferent
projections from the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices to
the neocortex are indeed extremely widespread (Van Hoesen,
1982). Recent analyses (Lavenex et al., 1998; Lavenex and Amaral,

unpublished observations) have shown a relatively high degree of
reciprocity of the connections between the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices and the neocortex. The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices receive convergent inputs from a variety of unimodal and polymodal association cortices in the temporal, frontal,
and parietal lobes (see above), and generally appear to project back
to the same unimodal and polymodal association cortices. This
degree of reciprocity seems to correspond, at first, to the pattern of
connectivity generally observed for other cortico-cortical projections (Felleman and Van Essen, 1991; Van Hoesen, 1982). The
reciprocity of the connections, however, is not absolute. Indeed,
the neocortical cells that originate projections to the perirhinal and
parahippocampal cortices may not necessarily be the same cells in
particular cortical regions that receive the feedback projections
(Lavenex and Amaral, unpublished observations). Moreover, there
are clear discrepancies in the reciprocity of the connections between the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices and the neocortex. The efferent projections from the perirhinal cortex to the
frontal cortex, for instance, are much more limited and reach fewer
areas than those that send projections to the perirhinal cortex.
Similarly, the afferent projections from the superior temporal sulcus arise from a much wider area and are much more important
than the efferent projections from the perirhinal cortex to the
superior temporal sulcus. In contrast, the perirhinal cortex receives
relatively few projections from the caudal visual areas V4 and
TEO, and appears to send more widespread projections to these
areas. These findings have important implications for the models
of interaction between the hippocampus and neocortex and longterm storage of information in the neocortex.

**Laminar** **Organization**

The laminar distribution of the projections from one cortical
area to another has been previously used to define hierarchical
relationships in the visual system (Felleman and Van Essen, 1991).
Cortico-cortical connections can be divided into three different
categories, based on layers of origin and termination: feedforward
(or ascending) projections, lateral (or local) projections, and feedback (or descending) projections (Rockland and Pandya, 1979).
Despite modifications of the definitions necessitated by the periallocortical cytoarchitecture of the entorhinal cortex, the projections
from the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices to the entorhinal cortex appear to exhibit a feedforward (or ascending) type of
organization. The projections from the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices originate primarily from the superficial layer III
and from layer V. Since the entorhinal cortex does not have a
typical layer IV (internal granular layer), these feedforward projections terminate primarily in the more superficial layers II and III,
that in turn project forward to the dentate gyrus and hippocampus.
The organization of these feedforward (ascending) projections is
thus typical of a hierarchical organization of these different cortical
areas, with the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices located at
a lower level than the entorhinal cortex.

The efferent connections from the hippocampal formation to
the neocortex indicate that these projections are typical of feedback
(or descending) projections. Indeed, projections from CA1 and the

**FLOW** **OF** **INFORMATION** **WITHIN** **THE**

**NEOCORTICAL-HIPPOCAMPAL** **LOOP**

The connectional anatomy described above tells us little about
function itself. But it certainly helps to define the structural bound

aries within which functional mechanisms must operate. Considering the neocortical-hippocampal loop, it is the hierarchical organization of associational networks that most uniquely characterizes
the neuroanatomy of the structures forming the medial temporal
lobe.

Previous models of the role of the hippocampus in memory
consolidation generally considered the medial temporal lobe as a
whole and neglected these “intermediate” cortices (i.e., the entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices) that were used as
mere relays to transfer information from the neocortex to the hippocampus and vice versa. Thus, schemes describing the flow of
information through the medial temporal memory system usually
represented information transmitted from the neocortex to the
hippocampal complex and back out from the hippocampal complex to the neocortex. Functional studies, however, support the
interpretation derived from the neuroanatomical data briefly described above, that these “intermediate” cortices do not act as mere

**Perirhinal** **and** **Parahippocampal** **Cortices,** **the**
**First** **Level** **of** **Integration**

The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices represent the
first stage in the integration of multimodal polysensory information within the medial temporal lobe memory system. Early
electrophysiological studies demonstrated that single neurons
in the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices are responsive to
multimodal stimulation (Desimone and Gross, 1979). More
recent electrophysiological studies have demonstrated the importance of the perirhinal cortex in the establishment of memory traces in the neocortex. In rats, perirhinal cortex activation
appears to promote enhancement in cortico-cortical pathways
secondarily activated by perirhinal inputs (Ivanco et al., 1996).
In monkeys, Higuchi and Miyashita (1996) demonstrated the

role of the perirhinal cortex in the formation of mnemonic
neuronal responses to visual paired associates in the inferotemporal cortex (TE). In their study, efferent projections from the
entorhinal and/or perirhinal cortices were found to be necessary
for the maintenance or consolidation of pair-coding information in area TE. Indeed, the ability of inferotemporal neurons
to represent associations between picture pairs was lost after
neurotoxin lesions of the entorhinal and perirhinal cortices,
most likely through disruption of feedback neural signals to the
inferotemporal neurons. Neuronal response patterns of neurons
in the perirhinal cortex may extend well beyond sensory activation. In the delayed-matching-to-sample task, the pattern of
activity of perirhinal cells during the delay (when no stimulus is
present) reflects the memory of the sample stimulus (Miller et
al., 1993; Miyashita and Chang, 1988), although the presentation of another nonmatching stimulus abolishes this delay activity (Miller et al., 1993). This suggests that the delay activity
in perirhinal cells represents the neuronal response corresponding to the maintenance of an explicit representation of the
stimulus held in memory (Miyashita and Chang, 1988).

A study of visual recognition recently showed that lesions of the
perirhinal cortex produce a deficit nearly as severe as that found
after entorhinal plus perirhinal cortex lesions, whereas ablations of
the entorhinal cortex alone produce only a mild deficit (Meunier et
al., 1993). Similarly, Buckley and Gaffan (1998) and Buckley et al.
(1997) showed in different tasks that the function of the perirhinal
cortex can be dissociated from the function of the other structures
of the medial temporal lobe, although the exact nature of the tasks
and thus the function of the perirhinal cortex remain a matter of
debate.

Taken together, these data indicate that perirhinal neurons are
multimodal and may be coding more than sensory attributes. The
precise characterization of the response properties of perirhinal
neurons requires substantial additional research. It is not clear, for
example, if there are topographic differences in response attributes.
It will also be important, albeit difficult, to determine how dependent the perirhinal cortex is on the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus for the function(s) that it carries out.

**Entorhinal** **Cortex,** **the** **Second** **Level** **of**
**Integration**

The entorhinal cortex represents the second stage in the integration of multimodal polysensory information within the medial
temporal lobe system. Electrophysiological studies have shown
that visually responsive cells and stimulus-selective cells are less
common in the entorhinal cortex than in the perirhinal cortex
(Suzuki et al., 1997). This suggests that cells in the entorhinal
cortex are less involved in the analysis and coding of object/sensory
features than the perirhinal cortex. Given the hierarchical organization of associational connectivity described above, entorhinal
neurons might respond solely to the conjoint activation by neurons
from several cortical areas, i.e., conjunction of information from
separate multimodal polysensory areas, and not to the unique activation by neurons from a single cortical area, i.e., a simple stimulus. However, cells in the entorhinal cortex do respond differen

tially to different objects, suggesting that some object information
is retained in the entorhinal cortex and is presumably passed on to
subsequent structures within the hippocampal formation.

**The** **Hippocampal** **Formation,** **the** **Ultimate** **Level**
**of** **Integration**

As the ultimate structure along the neocortical-hippocampal
loop, the hippocampal complex participates in the highest level of
integration of information. It receives only highly integrated supermodal or amodal information from the entorhinal, perirhinal,
and parahippocampal cortices and sends back even more highly
processed, abstract, multidimensional information to the structures that originated these projections. Others have suggested that
it is only after reaching the hippocampus that the memory representation attains the full richness that characterizes declarative
memory (Squire, 1992; Cohen and Eichenbaum, 1993).

**Timing** **of** **Information** **Processing**

Given the neuroanatomical characteristics of the structures
forming the medial temporal lobe (hierarchical organization, high
level of associativity, and reciprocity of connections), it might seem
difficult to distinguish between the different functional properties
of these structures. Given the extreme interconnectivity of these
structures, how can one try to determine the type of information
processing that takes place at one particular level, independently
from the other structures that interact with it? A solution to this
problem might be to consider the timing of neuronal responses
within the network. Transfer of information from one stage to the
next along the neocortical-hippocampal loop is necessarily associated with delays. As one progresses from the neocortex to the
hippocampus, neuronal response patterns should increase in informational complexity. At the same time, feedback projections send
back a more integrated signal to the areas that preceded them.
Consequently, one might be able to see neurons, located at the
early stages of the loop, conveying different types of information in
their firing patterns, starting at different latencies. Such a hypothesis is derived from an experiment by Sugase et al. (1999), who
recorded single neurons in the superior temporal sulcus of the
monkey. They demonstrated that single neurons conveyed two
different scales of facial information in their firing patterns, starting at different latencies. At an early time point, the informational
content of the neuronal responses could differentiate categories of
faces (monkey vs. human) but not individuals of the category. At a
later time point, neuronal responses had sufficient information to
differentiate individuals within a category. This differential pattern
of firing was thought to be due to the interaction with higher-order
cortical areas through feedback projections. If each different stage
of the neocortical-hippocampal loop processes different levels of
integration of information, time-delay might also permit feedback
contributions to multiple levels of coding by the same neurons in
the early stages of the loop.

**CONCLUSIONS**

The structures forming the medial temporal lobe, including the
dentate gyrus, hippocampus, subiculum, presubiculum, parasubiculum, and entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices, appear to be necessary for the establishment of long-term
declarative memory. These structures are thought to be involved in
the “consolidation” of information in higher-order associational
cortices via feedback projections. The neocortical-hippocampal
loop is most uniquely characterized by the fact that it is a hierarchical organization of associational networks. These cortices form
a hierarchy of associativity that increases in complexity when going
from the neocortex to the hippocampal complex. Indeed, associational connections within the perirhinal, parahippocampal, and
entorhinal cortices and the convergence/divergence of inputs at
different levels of the loop enable a significant amount of integration of polysensory and multimodal inputs well before information
reaches the hippocampus.

The perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices receive convergent
inputs from a variety of unimodal and polymodal association cortices in the temporal, frontal, and parietal lobes. The perirhinal and
parahippocampal cortices represent the first stage of this hierarchy
of associational networks participating in the integration of multimodal polysensory information within the medial temporal lobe
memory system. Together, the perirhinal and parahippocampal
cortices constitute the major sources of cortical input to the monkey entorhinal cortex, the second stage of the neocortical-hippocampal loop. The entorhinal cortex represents a hierarchically
higher level of associativity, where the important network of intrinsic, associational connections further increases the level of integration of information before it is transmitted to the hippocampus. Thus, while the hippocampal formation appears to receive
direct inputs from a relatively limited number of neocortical regions, each of these regions is a convergence zone that likely provides information from a much broader extent of cortex. In this
way, the hippocampal formation is ultimately linked to much of
the processing that takes place within the neocortex.

The connections between the neocortex and the structures
forming the medial temporal lobe are generally reciprocal. The
afferent connections from the neocortex to the hippocampal complex indicate that these projections are typical of feedforward (or
ascending) projections. The efferent connections from the hippocampal complex to the neocortex indicate that these projections
are typical of feedback (or descending) projections. The connections between the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices and the
hippocampal formation are highly reciprocal. Accordingly, the
perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices play a unique role in the
dissemination of hippocampal output. The connections between
the perirhinal and parahippocampal cortices and the neocortex also
show a relatively high degree of reciprocity. However, there may
not be a precise point-to-point relationship in the reciprocity of
these connections. Moreover, there are clear discrepancies in the
reciprocity of the connections, in particular between the perirhinal
cortex and some areas in the frontal and temporal lobes. Together,
these findings have important implications for the models of interaction between the hippocampus and neocortex and the long-term
storage of information in the neocortex. One implication of this
system is that the level of integration or abstraction of information
reaches its highest state in the hippocampal formation, and this
high-level abstraction is then returned widely to the neocortex.
Whether each recipient cortical region uses this return information
in a similar way or perhaps has a mechanism to extract some information while discarding the remainder will be of fundamental
interest to models of the medial temporal lobe in memory function.
