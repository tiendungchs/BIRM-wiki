## Abstract

Long-term memory formation is a major function of sleep. Based on evidence from neurophysiological and behavioral studies mainly in humans and rodents, we consider the formation of long-term memory during sleep as an active systems consolidation process that is embedded in a process of global synaptic downscaling. Repeated neuronal replay of representations originating from the hippocampus during slow-wave sleep leads to a gradual transformation and integration of representations in neocortical networks. We highlight three features of this process: (i) hippocampal replay that, by capturing episodic memory aspects, drives consolidation of both hippocampus-dependent and non-hippocampus-dependent memory; (ii) brain oscillations hallmarking slow-wave and rapid-eye movement sleep that provide mechanisms for regulating both information flow across distant brain networks and local synaptic plasticity; and (iii) qualitative transformations of memories during systems consolidation resulting in abstracted, gist-like representations.

## Main

The phenomenon of sleep preserving memory from forgetting has long been recognized. One of the first experimental studies demonstrating a beneficial effect of sleep on memory was performed in 1924 by Jenkins and Dallenbach, who repeatedly tested two young men after learning lists of nonsense syllables [^1]. Recall of the syllables was distinctly better when the participants had slept after the encoding session than if they stayed awake. Since then, the memory-promoting effect of sleep has been confirmed in numerous studies (for reviews see refs. [^2] [^3] [^4]). Initially, the effect was explained by the protective effect sleep has on newly encoded and still labile memories, preventing them from being overwritten by new information (retroactive interference). More recent accounts considering the underlying neuronal mechanism and, in particular, the neuronal replay of memories during sleep, emphasize an active role of sleep in memory consolidation [^2] [^4] [^5]. In this Review, we will first introduce the ‘active systems consolidation’ concept, which is the currently most integrative account of sleep-dependent memory consolidation. We next discuss the role of neuronal replay in memory consolidation. We then discuss synaptic rescaling during sleep and consider how sleep oscillations regulate systems consolidation. We highlight recent evidence for a distinct role of the thalamus in sleep-dependent consolidation and, finally, discuss behavioral studies in humans that indicate how consolidation during sleep transforms memory representations.

## Active systems consolidation

Consolidation is the process that transforms newly encoded representations or engrams (see Box [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec3)), which are labile and prone to decay, into more stable long-term memories. Active systems consolidation accounts for long-term memory formation in the hippocampus-dependent episodic memory system, with a particular focus on the role of sleep (Box [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec4)). When an episode is encoded, different brain areas host components of the experience, for instance, the faces and voices one encounters in the context of a scientific meeting. The hippocampus is assumed to bind these components into one unique episodic memory [^6] [^7]. Consequently, the memory starts out highly dependent on the hippocampus (Fig. [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Fig1)). During sleep, the hippocampal neuronal representation is repeatedly reactivated, with these reactivations propagating across the associated memory network and simultaneously reactivating its distributed neocortical components [^2]. Synaptic consolidation processes triggered by this coactivation strengthen the memory representations in the neocortex and thus allow the slowly learning neocortex to integrate them into pre-existing long-term memories. Importantly, the systems consolidation process during sleep does not simply increase the long-term stability of memories. Because it reorganizes the memory, with the neocortex integrating many related and overlapping experiences, consolidation also yields representations at higher levels of abstraction, generalization, and efficiency [^8]. Studies using functional MRI (fMRI) in humans have indeed shown that sleep after encoding supports the ‘corticalization’ of memories [^9] [^10]. In one of these studies [^9], the effects emerged over a period of 6 months after the post-encoding night of sleep, underscoring the long timescale for the corticalization of memories.

Fig. 1: Memory reactivations strengthen neocortical representations.

Notably, although the active systems consolidation concept originally described hippocampus-dependent memory, it likewise accounts for memories that are classified as ‘non-hippocampus-dependent’ [^11]. For example, a recent study in rats tested the effects of sleep on novel-object recognition memory, the encoding and retrieval of which essentially rely on the perirhinal cortex and not the hippocampus [^12]. Compared with wakefulness, sleep in the 2-h post-encoding interval enhanced object-recognition memories. This enhancement only emerged 2 weeks after the encoding session, was associated with signs of enhanced memory processing (spindle activity) during post-encoding sleep and, importantly, required hippocampal activity during post-encoding sleep, but not during remote recall (the latter indicates that these memories did not depend on the hippocampus for retrieval). Hippocampal involvement in the sleep-dependent consolidation of non-hippocampus-dependent memory has also been revealed by human fMRI studies, in which hippocampal activity during motor-skill training predicted performance gains over a subsequent period of nocturnal sleep [^13].

## Neuronal ensemble reactivation

Replay of firing patterns in hippocampal neuron ensembles during sleep is a key feature of active systems consolidation, as it is thought to cause the gradual formation of stable representations in extra-hippocampal networks (Box [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec4)). Such reactivation is most consistently observed during slow-wave sleep (SWS), but also occurs during quiet wakefulness [^14] [^15]. Early findings in rats showed reactivation during SWS after performing simple spatial tasks (such as running on a track): specifically, re-emergence of activity in single hippocampal place cells [^16], correlated firing activity between pairs of hippocampal place cells [^17] and preservation of the temporal order of firing observed during encoding [^18] [^19]. The replay is accompanied by sharp-wave ripples [^20] [^21]. Disrupting these ripples impairs subsequent recall, indicating their causal role in consolidation [^22]. Neuronal ensemble reactivation cannot be reliably detected in rapid eye-movement (REM) sleep [^20] [^23].

In humans, fMRI and electroencephalogram (EEG) studies provide indirect evidence for memory replay during sleep and its benefits for consolidation [^24] [^25]. Further support comes from intracranial recordings in patients with epilepsy. For example, Zhang et al.[^26] show that stimulus-specific gamma-band patterns during the encoding of pictures are reactivated by ripples during subsequent sleep. Interestingly, the ripple-triggered replay of late (occurring 500–1,200 ms post-encoding) and early (occurring 100–500 ms post-encoding) gamma activity is associated with later recall and forgetting of the items, respectively.

Neuronal ensemble reactivations can also occur in cortical and other subcortical brain regions, including prefrontal [^27], parietal [^28], motor [^29], visual [^19], entorhinal cortex [^30], ventral striatum [^31], and the ventral tegmental area (VTA) [^32] [^33]. Simultaneous recordings from multiple brain regions show that hippocampal reactivations occur in coordination with neuronal firing in the neocortex [^19] [^34], striatum [^31], amygdala [^35], and VTA [^33], often with a temporal delay (of 40–50 ms) between hippocampus and other areas [^19] [^27] [^31]. The time delays suggest that ripple-associated memory reactivations originating in the hippocampus spread to neocortical and striatal areas. In a repetitive process, this might mediate the gradual redistribution of representations, by enhancing connectivity in extra-hippocampal ensembles. However, extra-hippocampal ensemble reactivation can occur also independently of hippocampal reactivation, even in structures as closely linked to hippocampal output as the medial entorhinal cortex [^30]. Reactivations were also found to be synchronized across several neocortical areas [^36].

### Replay during wakefulness

Hippocampal neuronal ensemble reactivations occur conjointly with ripples during quiet wakefulness and intermittent rest periods during task performance as well [^21]. This ‘wake replay’ has been related to short-term retrieval and memory-guided decision making [^15]. Importantly, reactivations during wakefulness take place under enhanced acetylcholinergic activity, which is known to suppress hippocampal output from CA1 neurons, whereas SWS occurs under minimum cholinergic activity and therefore less inhibition of hippocampal output [^37] [^38]. Consequently, information flow from hippocampus to extra-hippocampal networks is enhanced during SWS, whereas cortical information flow toward the hippocampus predominates during waking [^39]. Accordingly, wake reactivations in the hippocampus appear to be less synchronized with reactivation in extra-hippocampal regions [^40]. Nevertheless, such wake reactivations may also contribute to the consolidation and corticalization of memories [^41], although the underlying mechanisms presumably differ.

We next discuss the conditions that initialize hippocampal replay, and how this replay might form more-persisting representations outside the hippocampus.

### Which memories are reactivated during sleep?

Although hippocampal reactivations during sleep are not connected to an acute behavioral context (unlike wake reactivations), their occurrence is linked to various aspects of the encoding situation, such as the depth of encoding and salience of the encoded information. For example, reactivations are stronger—i.e., co-firing patterns show greater similarity to those during wake encoding—for locations an animal has explored more intensely and for experiences in novel environments compared to familiar ones [^42], in part due to more-coordinated hippocampal firing during encoding [^43]. Replay after novelty exposure also remains detectable for longer than replay after exposure to a familiar environment [^17] [^19] [^44] and is enhanced for locations associated with an aversive emotional response [^35] or reward [^45].

The mechanisms conveying these peri-encoding influences on later sleep reactivations are not well understood, but may involve theta activity and dopaminergic activity. Theta activity is enhanced during encoding of salient information [^46]. While learning place–reward associations, hippocampal place cells and reward-coding neurons in the ventral striatum show synchronized activity in the theta band, and the strength of this synchronization is associated with the strength of joint replay of these cells during subsequent SWS [^31]. Likewise, rats learning a rule on a Y-maze task show enhanced theta coherence in hippocampal–prefrontal cortex at the decision point of the maze [^47], and prefrontal cortical neuron ensembles firing at this decision point are more likely to reactivate during subsequent SWS. This study additionally suggests that increased prefrontal–hippocampal theta coherence is due to reward-related dopaminergic inputs to prefrontal areas [^47]. Dopamine can indeed tag synapses for later strengthening [^48]. The hippocampus receives dopaminergic input from the VTA, and stimulating dopaminergic neurons in the VTA during encoding of a new spatial memory increased reactivation of respective hippocampal neuron ensembles during both subsequent sleep and later memory performance [^49]. There is conflicting evidence on whether the VTA dopamine neurons also show reactivation during subsequent sleep [^32] [^33], but stimulating putatively dopaminergic reward circuits in the medial forebrain bundle upon firing of a specific hippocampal place cell during sleep led to a distinct behavioral preference for the respective place field in rats [^50].

The above data suggest that enhanced theta coherence and enhanced dopaminergic activity at encoding may contribute to tagging newly encoded representations for privileged consolidation during sleep; dopaminergic reactivation during sleep may further augment memory but is not required for consolidation. From a more general perspective, the findings discussed here indicate that ensemble reactivations in hippocampal networks can be triggered by parts of the representation residing in cortical networks (see below) and in other networks, such as reward circuits of the VTA and/or emotion-related circuits of the amygdala.

## Synaptic rescaling during sleep

The systems consolidation of freshly encoded memories during sleep also involves a reorganization and redistribution of connectivity in the cell ensembles hosting the representation. This process entails opposing changes at the synaptic level: parts of the neuronal ensembles that initially encode the experience will reduce their contributions to memory storage, leading to a weakening and downscaling of synapses; at the same time, other parts of the engram will maintain or even increase their contribution to the memory representation, implicating the selective strengthening and upscaling of synaptic connections (including the formation of new synapses). Sleep can promote both synaptic upscaling and downscaling [^51].

The processes of systems consolidation are thus embedded in a global homeostatic regulation of synaptic connectivity, which is another fundamental function of sleep, according to the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis [^52]. This theory proposes that encoding of information during wakefulness induces a global upscaling of glutamatergic synapses in cortical and subcortical networks, including the hippocampus, whereas during subsequent sleep, average connectivity in these networks is downscaled to baseline levels to prevent an accumulation of upscaling that would lead to excessive space and energy demands. In comparison with wakefulness, sleep indeed seems to reduce the overall number of synapses in the cortex. A study in adult mice revealed that sleep globally decreases the area of contact between cortical axon terminals and dendritic spines [^53]. At the molecular level, sleep reduces synaptic AMPA receptor levels [^54] [^55], which are strongly correlated with functional synaptic strength.

Functional parameters also appear to reflect a net synaptic downscaling over the course of sleep. For example, firing rates of cortical and hippocampal neurons decrease during sleep in rodents [^56] [^57] [^58], and in humans, cortical excitability decreases over the course of sleep [^59] [^60]. However, these findings remain inconclusive: changes over the course of sleep could reflect cell-intrinsic rather than synaptic forms of plasticity or a shift toward increased inhibition in the cortical excitation–inhibition balance rather than global downscaling of excitatory synapses [^61].

Although an average synaptic downscaling is compatible with systems consolidation, central to consolidation is that synaptic ensembles hosting critical memory information are preserved or even upscaled. Indeed, structural imaging of synapses shows that the overall decrease in synapse numbers and the area of synaptic contact during sleep co-occurs with local increases in these measures as well as the emergence of new spines [^53] [^55] [^62] [^63]. Likewise, firing rates of distinct subgroups of cells in visual cortex remain stable or increase during sleep after monocular deprivation [^64].

### Contribution of sleep stages to synaptic scaling

Both SWS and REM sleep may be involved in synaptic scaling. Regarding SWS, on the one hand, the extent of EEG slow oscillatory activity during SWS is linked to global synaptic downscaling, as reflected by a decrease in cortical excitability during sleep (for example, refs. [^54] [^65]). On the other hand, structural imaging in mice indicates that SWS after training on a rotarod motor task not only protects newly formed spines in a subset of cells in the motor cortex, but also promotes the formation of new spines, with this process depending on the reactivation of the cells during SWS [^62]. Another study reveals a long-term potentiation-like mechanism that increases cortical somatosensory evoked responses during periods of natural SWS, as well as during slow oscillatory activity induced through thalamic 1-Hz stimulation [^66].

As for REM sleep, neuronal firing rates in hippocampal and cortical networks [^57] [^67] were found to decrease more during REM than SWS. These decreases may reflect synaptic downscaling, but could also reflect the generally enhanced cortical inhibition that characterizes REM sleep [^61]. However, morphological imaging in mice confirmed a prevailing synaptic pruning in cortex during REM sleep [^63]. In this study, REM sleep, compared with REM deprivation, is associated with an increased fraction of the spines that were newly formed following a prior motor task training that increase their size during sleep. However, REM sleep concurrently enhances the overall elimination of newly formed spines in the motor cortex [^63]. Blocking calcium spikes during REM sleep blocks both spine strengthening and elimination [^63]. Thus, REM sleep may play a stronger role than SWS in global synaptic downscaling and may simultaneously protect selected newly formed synapses from being downscaled. By contrast, SWS might primarily strengthen synaptic connectivity of engram ensembles and have a less pronounced role in enabling global synaptic downscaling.

Intriguingly, the studies discussed above [^62] [^63] reveal that regulation of synaptic scaling during post-encoding SWS and REM sleep pertains to specific branches of dendrites of excitatory cortical cells rather than to single localized synapses or to the whole dendritic tree. This suggests that dendritic branches represent computational elements mediating between synaptic and cellular plasticity [^68].

## Sleep oscillations time systems consolidation

The interplay and coupling between different EEG and local field potential rhythms have been identified as a key mechanisms for the communication between distributed networks in the awake brain [^69], and likewise, they regulate the communication between hippocampus, neocortex and other extra-hippocampal structures during sleep [^70] [^71] (Fig. [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Fig2)). As discussed below, this oscillatory regulation enables the timing of neural reactivations originating in hippocampal networks, such that they effect synaptic changes in selected cortical areas.

### Oscillations during slow wave sleep

The oscillations dominating SWS are the <1 Hz slow oscillation (SO), ~12–15 Hz spindles, and ~80 Hz ripples (Box [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec14); frequencies refer to those found in the human brain; for species differences see Box [4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec15)). SOs represent neuronal network alternations between down-states of synchronized membrane hyperpolarization and up-states of synchronized depolarization. They are generated in cortical networks (layers 2/3 and 5), migrate mainly from anterior to posterior regions, and also reach subcortical structures including the hippocampus [^72] [^73] [^74]. Human studies have provided evidence for a causal contribution of SOs to memory consolidation. These studies showed that non-invasive brain stimulation applied to increase slow oscillatory activity during post-encoding SWS enhanced the memory-promoting effect of sleep [^75] [^76] [^77].

Spindles are generated in the thalamus as a result of an interaction between networks of the nucleus reticularis (TRN) and other thalamic nuclei (Box [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec14)). Spindles spread to the entire neocortex via thalamocortical fibers, but also reach the hippocampus [^78]. Their neocortical occurrence typically pertains to more localized networks than that of SOs. Spindle activity is increased during sleep after learning, particularly over cortical areas engaging in the encoding process, and spindle density correlates with post-sleep retrieval performance [^79] [^80] [^81]. Electrically inducing spindle-like firing patterns facilitates synaptic long-term potentiation in cortical neurons [^82], probably by increasing intracellular calcium concentrations. During sleep spindles, apical dendrites of cortical pyramidal cells in mice show increased local calcium activity [^83] [^84]. Altogether, spindles are a candidate mechanism for the construction of long-term representations in cortical networks.

The depolarizing phase of SOs drives the emergence of thalamic spindles [^85] such that these spindles tend to nest in the up-state of the SOs [^86]. Prior learning enhances the SO–spindle coupling [^87]. SO–spindle coupling appears to facilitate synaptic plasticity in cortical pyramidal cells (Fig. [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Fig3)). Two-photon calcium imaging in naturally sleeping mice reveals that during SO–spindle events, pyramidal cells, along with parvalbumin-positive interneurons that convey a strong somatic inhibition of these cells, showed maximum activity. Somatostatin-positive interneurons, typically inhibiting the pyramidal dendrites, showed low activity [^88]. Thus, when spindles nest in the SO up-state, cortical pyramidal cells appear to be most sensitive to specific input to their dendrites (originating, for instance, from hippocampal memory replay), while the concurrent inhibition of their soma prevents excess firing activity. Indeed, the same cortical circuit conditions have been found to support plasticity during wakefulness [^88].

Fig. 3: Conditions of facilitated synaptic plasticity in cortical pyramidal cells when spindles couple with slow oscillation.

Other studies confirmed that spindles nesting in SO up-states are most effective in enhancing consolidation. Spindles induced by optogenetic 8-Hz stimulation of the rostral TRN during post-learning SWS enhanced context-conditioned fear memory only when the spindles were induced in phase-lock with spontaneously occurring SO up-states [^71]. The memory improvement observed after experimental closed-loop stimulation of SOs during post-learning sleep in humans might be a secondary effect of the stimulated SOs that—like natural SOs—drives the occurrence of spindles phase-locked to their up-state [^76] [^89]. There is evidence that an SO without a nested spindle primarily unfolds depotentiating effects on cortical synapses [^90]. The diminished overnight memory consolidation found in older human participants was correlated with mistimed coupling of spindles to the SO up-state [^70]. Conversely, transcranial direct-current stimulation of slow oscillatory activity in patients with mild cognitive impairment improved both SO–spindle coupling and memory performance after sleep [^91]. In sum, the coupling of spindles to the SO up-state appears to be central to the induction of synaptic plasticity underlying the formation of longer-term representations in cortical networks.

Hippocampal ripples are the third oscillatory phenomenon that occurs mainly during SWS, but also during quiet wakefulness (Box [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec14)). Hippocampal ripples occur superimposed on a large depolarizing sharp wave [^92]. They increase after learning, accompany the forward replay of newly encoded memories during SWS and predict later recall (see above). Recent evidence suggests that ripples in hippocampal networks act simultaneously to downscale synapses in these ensembles [^93]. Their primary function thus appears to pertain to the temporal organization of neuronal ensemble reactivation in the hippocampal CA1 subregion, from where reactivations spread to medial entorhinal, striatal and cortical areas. Ripple-like activity identified in cortical regions [^94] possibly reflects such coordinated hippocampal–neocortical replay. Accordingly, the coupling of hippocampal with neocortical ripples in rats increases during sleep after the encoding of a spatial maze [^94].

In the hippocampus, ripples tend to nest into the excitable troughs of spindles, thereby forming ‘spindle–ripple events’ that have been proposed as a mechanism that facilitates hippocampus-to-neocortical transmission of reactivated memory information [^86] [^95]. Hippocampal ripple activity is synchronized to the SO, i.e., it is suppressed during the SO down-state and enhanced during the up-state [^71] [^86]. The coupling with thalamocortical SO–spindle events appears to be tightest in central-parietal cortical areas. However, a time-delayed coupling of hippocampal ripples with SO–spindle events (with the ripple preceding the SO–spindle by ~130 ms) has also been observed in medial prefrontal cortex, considered a hub area in the hippocampus-to-neocortical redistribution of representations [^96].

### Regulation of oscillation coupling during SWS

Based on their precise temporal relationships and evidence from selective stimulation of SOs and spindles [^71] [^87] [^89] [^96] [^97] a loop-like scenario can be drawn (Fig. [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Fig2)): top-down, the neocortical SO, through its inactivating hyperpolarizing down-state, sets a time frame for a window of information transfer and plasticity that is opened with the succeeding depolarizing up-state. The SO up-state drives the thalamus to generate spindles, which subsequently synchronize hippocampal ripples (and enwrapped ensemble reactivations) to their excitable troughs. Bottom-up, hippocampal ripples can directly induce SOs in the medial prefrontal cortex [^96]. There is also evidence that thalamic spindles feed back to cortical networks to facilitate, possibly through increased intracellular calcium concentrations, the emergence of SOs in cortical neuron populations [^71] [^87] —an influence that might be particularly relevant when several SO cycles occur in a row. On the other side, refractoriness of the spindle-generating mechanism might be a major factor determining the fading of cycling activity in this loop [^89] [^98].

Prior learning increases coupling of the three rhythms, and increased coupling of rhythms during post-encoding sleep predicts enhanced performance at later recall [^71] [^96]. A recent study [^99] shows that the triple-coupling of rhythms during SWS indeed goes along with temporally coordinated firing activity in cortex and hippocampus, although these experiments did not record concurrent cortical field potentials. The authors trained rats on a sound-guided task and found that during subsequent sleep, patterned activity occurring in the auditory cortex preceded (by ~200 ms) and predicted patterned activity accompanied by ripples in the hippocampus. In turn, hippocampal patterns during ripples predicted subsequent activity patterns (~200 ms) in auditory cortex. Importantly, delivering the task-related sounds during sleep revealed that patterns of activity in the auditory cortex can inform the hippocampus about which memory to reactivate (see also ref. [^100]). A similar loop-like interaction is observed between the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) and hippocampus, with ACC neuronal firing preceding hippocampal ripples and a subpopulation of ACC neurons showing further activation immediately after ripple activity [^101].

Altogether, these findings support the notion of a cortical–hippocampal–cortical loop of information processing which, in top-down direction, is relayed by the thalamus and coordinates the hippocampus-to-neocortical transmission of reactivated memory information while simultaneously allowing synaptic consolidation processes to store this information for longer terms in neocortical networks. The top-down coordination not only determines the timing of hippocampal memory replay—i.e., when the cortex is in an excitable up-state—but also appears to provide information as to which memory is reactivated.

### Oscillations during REM sleep

REM sleep in rodents is characterized by an abundance of theta activity (Box [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec14)). In human EEG traces, theta activity is far less prominent (for species differences, see Box [4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec15)), but a network oscillating in the 4–8 Hz theta rhythm during REM sleep has been identified in human neocortex, including the ACC and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, as well as the hippocampus [^102]. REM sleep theta activity has been specifically linked to the consolidation of emotional memories (for example, refs. [^103] [^104]). In rats, enhanced coherence of theta activity during post-encoding REM sleep in a network spanning the amygdala, hippocampus, and medial prefrontal cortex is associated with enhanced recall of Pavlovian cued conditioned fear responses [^105]. Induction of hippocampal theta activity by optogenetic stimulation of parvalbumin-positive interneurons compensated for the adverse effects of sleep deprivation on the consolidation of fear memories in mice [^106]. Collectively, these observations concur with the notion that REM-sleep theta activity contributes to a systems-consolidation process in networks specifically engaging in the processing of emotional stimuli.

REM theta activity may also contribute to non-emotional memory. For example, during REM sleep after running on a track, hippocampal place cells encoding a novel location tend to reactivate during the peak of REM theta, whereas the same cells change their preferred reactivation phase to the REM theta trough once the environment became familiar [^107]. Thus, the novelty of the engram determines the phase relationship of its reactivation along the theta cycle. Reactivation during the theta peak is expected to enhance a memory trace, whereas reactivation during the trough is assumed to result in weakening of the trace [^107]. Also, optogenetic suppression of septo-hippocampal theta activity during REM sleep interferes with consolidation of spatial memories in a classical object–place recognition task [^108].

Natural REM sleep is always preceded by a period of non-REM sleep or SWS (Box [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Sec14)). Accordingly, the ‘sequential hypothesis’ postulates that REM sleep serves a function in memory formation that complements that of prior SWS [^109]. This view has received some support in recent studies. Analyzing triplets of SWS–REM–SWS episodes, Grosmark et al.[^57] found an overall reduction in firing rates in the hippocampus within triplets, together with an increased firing synchronized to ripples, possibly expressing memory replay. Notably, both firing rate reductions and synchronization with ripples were highly correlated with theta power in the interleaving REM sleep episode. In another study, the incidence of spindles and ripples during prior SWS were the strongest predictor of REM sleep-related decreases in hippocampal firing rates [^58]. Prior exploration of a novel environment increases the REM sleep-related expression of immediate early genes involved in synaptic plasticity in the neocortex of rats [^110], and this increase is strongly and positively correlated with the amplitude of prior sleep spindle activity [^110].

Together, the above findings support the idea that REM sleep acts on memory representations that were tagged by preceding ripples and spindles for further processing. During REM sleep, theta-phase-aligned neuronal reactivation might be a selection mechanism for which memory is going to be preserved. Suppressing this selection mechanism might disturb the consolidation of newly learned relevant memories by allowing consolidation of interfering irrelevant memories. Moreover, these findings seem to be consistent with morphological imaging data showing a predominant synaptic pruning during REM sleep of presumably ‘irrelevant’ synapses, sparing those newly formed spines that are essential for the representation [^63] and whose formation possibly causes enhanced immediate early gene levels during REM sleep [^110]. However, the immediate early gene-related changes are observed in the neocortex. Hence, the idea of a theta-associated selection mechanism between to-be-preserved and to-be-weakened memories, based on prior spindle and/or ripple-related tagging, remains speculative.

## Thalamus as a coordinator of consolidation during sleep

Thalamic contributions to memory consolidation during sleep are mainly considered in the context of sleep spindles. The prominent view originating from Steriade’s work [^85] is that thalamic spindles are under top-down control of the neocortical SO, which by its depolarizing phase drives the generation of spindles in the TRN. However, the thalamus and spindles might play a more leading role in the consolidation process, as discussed here.

Spindle activity is linked to sleep-dependent memory enhancements [^111], apparently more consistently so than SOs [^3]. In humans, spindles during non-REM sleep stage 2 occur in the absence of prominent SOs, and these spindles seem to support memory consolidation [^79]. During brain development, spindles emerge earlier than slow waves [^112] [^113], and sleep-dependent systems consolidation in human infants was found to be correlated with spindle activity rather than with slow oscillatory activity or delta waves [^80]. Moreover, sleep spindles are more localized than SOs in neocortical networks [^73], which may enable them to target confined networks supporting a specific memory [^65] [^114] [^115]. Interestingly, the location of an SO does not predict the neocortical location of the spindle nested in its up-state [^116], suggesting that mechanisms other than the SO inform spindles about the memory content to be enhanced.

The thalamus substantially contributes to the generation of all major sleep rhythms, including SOs [^111]. Optogenetically induced thalamic spindles elicit SOs within ~200 ms [^71] and simultaneously phase-lock ripples to their troughs in hippocampal networks. This spindle–ripple phase-locking is independent of whether the spindle was induced during or in the absence of an SO up-state, indicating direct thalamic control of ripple timing. However, a benefit of sleep in context-conditioned fear memory is observed only when spindles were induced during an SO up-state [^71]. These findings suggest that thalamic spindles are the primary factor regulating the timing of ripples and associated hippocampal memory replay. However, the effective transmission of reactivated memory to extra-hippocampal long-term stores requires that spindles occur during the excitable SO up-state.

A recent study [^117] provided initial insights into how the thalamus might regulate the consolidation of specific memories during sleep. Mice were exposed to a novel visual stimulus (a flickering grating of a specific orientation) to induce an orientation-specific response potentiation as a functional measure of plasticity. This response potentiation was observed as increased firing-rate responses in neurons of the lateral geniculate nucleus during the encoding session. Interestingly, in the visual cortex (V1), a parallel orientation-specific response potentiation emerges only after the animals sleep. During the sleep period, coherence between firing of lateral geniculate nucleus neurons and spindle oscillations and slow waves in V1 increases; optogenetic inhibition of V1 layer 6 corticothalamic neurons disrupt this coherence and block the sleep-dependent response enhancement in V1. These results indicate that sensory relay nuclei of the thalamus can quickly encode stimulus-specific information, which is then used to instruct the neocortex to form a corresponding memory during sleep. In this process, spindles and slow oscillatory activity coordinate the transmission of the memory from thalamus to neocortex. Future research should assess whether these results can be generalized to other sensory modalities and to the processing of more complex stimuli.

The idea of a leading role of the thalamus in memory consolidation during sleep has been corroborated by work indicating that the thalamic neuronal circuitry that generates spindles during sleep also critically contributes to the regulation of selective attention during wakefulness [^118] [^119]. Selective attention to specific sensory inputs is thought to involve prefrontal control over GABAergic TRN neurons, which—via feedforward inhibition on thalamocortical projection neurons—gate the information flow to the neocortex [^120]. Based on their predominant projections, Halassa et al.[^119] proposed to discriminate two broad categories of TRN neurons (see ref. [^121] for a related discrimination). Sensory-projecting TRN neurons have axon terminals primarily reaching sensory thalamic nuclei. In the wake attentive state, they synchronize their firing to an alpha rhythm, and the same neurons tend to synchronize their firing to spindle oscillations during sleep. Curiously, firing activity is locked to opposite phases of the spindle and alpha oscillations [^118]. In contrast, limbic-projecting TRN neurons exert inhibitory control over anterior thalamic nuclei, which are thought to mediate prefrontal–hippocampal interactions. Activity of limbic-projecting TRN neurons is not substantially modulated during sleep spindles or attention, but is strongly suppressed during SWS. The resulting release from TRN inhibition may facilitate offline processing in and information flow from limbic or hippocampal regions. Spindle power is positively and negatively correlated with firing rates of sensory-projecting and limbic-projecting TRN neurons, respectively, suggesting that these subnetworks may interact in regulating internal information flow during sleep-dependent memory processing [^119].

Overall, this work provides evidence for a common mechanism whereby GABAergic TRN neurons gate and select information flow toward the neocortex during wake attention and sleep spindles, suggesting the following simplified scenario: wake attention drives learning because higher-order cortices top-down select those neuronal networks best suited to improve task performance [^122]. This is accompanied by enhanced alpha activity in TRN sensory-projecting neurons and fast plasticity in networks of thalamic relay nuclei. Serving as attentional tags, these changes might facilitate the generation of spindles in the same local thalamic network during subsequent SWS, thereby promoting plasticity and memory consolidation in their neocortical target networks. In this scenario, spindles selectively connect distinct networks within neocortex and hippocampus in the same way that attentional mechanisms flexibly recruit widespread neuronal modules during wakefulness [^123].

This concept of a role for the thalamus in sleep-dependent memory formation is, of course, highly speculative. A most pressing question is how the thalamus, likely via the nucleus reuniens [^78], integrates hippocampal information into memory processing. Unlike their neocortical action, spindles reaching hippocampal networks, through the nesting of ripples and replay, would be expected to support synaptic downscaling and the fading of respective representations.

## Behavioral expression of consolidated memory

Systems consolidation during sleep implies that memory representations are gradually transformed at a rate that depends, among other things, on the type of information and the availability of knowledge schemas in long-term memory [^124]. Importantly, a transformation in the neuronal substrate of a memory during its ‘corticalization’ implies that the memory also changes in qualitative aspects, i.e., its content. Unfortunately, most studies on effects of sleep on memory performance assess only recall of a memory considered identical to what was encoded. Only a few studies, mostly in humans, have addressed possible sleep-dependent transformations in memory, focusing on two kinds of transformation, namely the abstraction of categories and rules, and the preferential consolidation of emotional over neutral information [^125]. Here we briefly discuss studies of the former kind of transformation.

The formation of generalized, schema-like memories in neocortical long-term stores is assumed to result from an abstraction process that accesses and integrates over time multiple episodes of single experiences with overlapping and shared features in hippocampal networks [^5] [^8]. Indeed, language studies in infants showed that sleep facilitates the emergence of generalized word categories [^80] [^126] (Fig. [4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-019-0467-3#Fig4)) and the abstraction of a grammatical structure [^127]. Similarly, in adults, sleep enhances category formation [^128] and learning of linguistic rules [^129]. The abstraction of sequence rules in serial motor tasks is linked to increased spindle and slow oscillatory activity during post-training non-REM sleep [^130] [^131]. In problem solving tasks, sleep enhances insights into hidden rules [^132] [^133] [^134]. However, these findings were not replicated in nap studies with shorter 3-h retention intervals [^135] [^136]. Also, studies using the Deese–Roediger–McDermott paradigm return inconsistent results: some studies report an enhancing effect of post-encoding sleep on semantic gist memory [^137] [^138], whereas in others, gist memory is even reduced after sleep [^139]. In a study using a visual version of the Deese–Roediger–McDermott paradigm, post-encoding sleep enhanced gist memory only at a test after 1 year, but not on the day after encoding [^131].

Overall, these results confirm that memory consolidation during post-encoding sleep can result in enhanced abstraction of gist in terms of categories and rules, which is consistent with the presumed neuronal mechanics of systems consolidation. Nevertheless, there are some contradictory results, possibly because some studies did not take into account the slow and variable time course of systems consolidation processes. Neocortical long-term stores might be able to quickly (within 24 h) assimilate newly encoded information only when suitable pre-existing schemas are available [^140]. Accordingly, enhancing effects of post-encoding sleep on abstraction processes that accompany the formation of extra-hippocampal representations should typically occur with delays substantially longer than a single day [^12] [^124].

A related factor is that little is known about the precise time course and boundary conditions of the fading of hippocampal representations. As long as an episodic hippocampal representation is intact, it might dominate over any newly transformed representation (i.e., outside the hippocampus) during recall. Indeed, behavioral studies using short retention intervals suggest that sleep, in the near term, even enhances episodic hippocampal representation, possibly reflecting a direct consequence of SWS-related memory replay in the hippocampus [^141]. Thus, in humans and rodents, post-encoding sleep is consistently found to strengthened the context-binding of an event at recall testing after short retention intervals [^141] [^142]; an enhancing effect of sleep on abstracted gist-like representations after a short retention interval might only be revealed if the task paradigm forces the participant to recall a context-independent (presumably non-hippocampal) representation [^143]. In this view, the decontextualization of a memory, strictly speaking, refers to the forgetting of contextual details, i.e., to the fading of the hippocampal representation. There is preliminary evidence that sleep accelerates such decontextualization in a manner that is only expressed after a longer delay (3 days) [^144]. However, such effects implicating sleep in processes of active forgetting, for example, through ripple-induced downscaling of hippocampal synapses [^93], need to be scrutinized in further studies.

## Conclusion

There is ample evidence that post-encoding sleep does not simply strengthen representations but qualitatively transforms memories in a way that enhances specific gist aspects. Sleep thereby aids the abstraction of more general, schema-like information. We argue that this transformative function of sleep, at the neuronal level, might be best explained by an active systems consolidation process. In this account, neuronal replay of newly encoded episodic representations originating from hippocampal networks during SWS promotes the gradual redistribution of connectivity and strengthening of representations in extra-hippocampal, predominantly neocortical networks. While systems consolidation during sleep appears to rely on similar subroutines of synaptic consolidation as those employed during wakefulness, the specific routing of information flow from hippocampal to neocortical networks and the resulting enhancement of cortical representations is a hallmark of consolidation during sleep. The interplay of field potential oscillations associated with SWS—specifically the nesting of replay-associated ripples into excitable troughs of spindles and the nesting of spindles into the excitable up-states of SOs—provides a mechanism that controls hippocampus-to-neocortical information transmission and the resulting plastic processes. Although this concept successfully integrates a large body of data, it remains tentative in many aspects as well. Most pressing questions pertain to the role of the thalamus in mediating systems consolidation during sleep and the gradual and variable time course of the corticalization of representations supported by sleep. Critically, these and other questions need to be scrutinized at the neurophysiological and behavioral levels in parallel.
