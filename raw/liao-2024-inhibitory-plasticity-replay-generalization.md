## Abstract

Memory consolidation assimilates recent experiences into long-term memory. This process requires the replay of learned sequences, although the content of these sequences remains controversial. Recent work has shown that the statistics of replay deviate from those of experience: stimuli that are experientially salient may be either recruited or suppressed from sharp-wave ripples. In this study, we found that this phenomenon can be explained parsimoniously and biologically plausibly by a Hebbian spike-time-dependent plasticity rule at inhibitory synapses. Using models at three levels of abstraction—leaky integrate-and-fire, biophysically detailed and abstract binary—we show that this rule enables efficient generalization, and we make specific predictions about the consequences of intact and perturbed inhibitory dynamics for network dynamics and cognition. Finally, we use optogenetics to artificially implant non-generalizable representations into the network in awake behaving mice, and we find that these representations also accumulate inhibition during sharp-wave ripples, experimentally validating a major prediction of our model. Our work outlines a potential direct link between the synaptic and cognitive levels of memory consolidation, with implications for both normal learning and neurological disease.

## Main

Humans and other animals have the remarkable ability to learn about the world from observations containing a mix of structural features of the environment and distractions that do not generalize. The ability to learn arbitrary sequences and relational maps, and to deploy them to guide behavior, is the defining computational function of the hippocampus [^1] [^2] [^3] [^4] [^5]. A subset of hippocampal neurons, termed ‘place cells’, exhibit selective tuning to discrete locations in space: as an animal explores its environment, the sequence of place cell firing reflects the animalʼs path [^6]. The hippocampus later reinstates previously learned sequences in temporally compressed ‘replay’ epochs inside high-frequency oscillatory events known as sharp-wave ripples (SWRs) [^7] during both sleep [^8] [^9] [^10] [^11] and awake rest [^12] [^13] [^14] [^15]. This replay is required for the long-term consolidation of memories, although how memories are selected for consolidation has been the subject of much debate [^16] [^17] [^18] [^19].

The strongly recurrently connected [^20] [^21] [^22] [^23] CA3 and CA2 regions of the hippocampus are thought to be the source of SWRs and the drivers of replay throughout the hippocampus [^7] [^24] [^25] [^26]. Despite this, replay has mostly been investigated within the hippocampal CA1 circuitry [^7] [^8] [^9] [^10] [^13] with fewer studies reporting replay in CA3/CA2 (refs. [^14] [^25] [^27] [^28]), largely due to practical constraints. Recurrent excitatory weight updates in CA3 occur by a symmetric spike-timing-dependent plasticity (sSTDP) rule [^29], providing a plausible explanation for the mix of forward and backward (temporally reversed) replay sequences observed experimentally [^13] [^30]. At the same time, modeling work has demonstrated the feasibility of obtaining spontaneous replay in biologically realistic spiking network models [^31] [^32] [^33].

Inhibition is also known to play a major role in SWR generation [^34] [^35], although comparatively little is known about inhibitory dynamics in CA3/CA2. Under the standard model of learning in neural circuits, Hebbian plasticity remodels excitatory–excitatory synapses against a relatively static inhibitory background [^36] [^37]. This model does not traditionally include plasticity at the GABAergic synapse, as a constant inhibitory background is presumed to be necessary for learning; however, this type of plasticity has been reported in vitro [^38] [^39], and a growing body of experimental and theoretical work has suggested a role for inhibitory plasticity in shaping network-level computations [^40] [^41] [^42] [^43] [^44] [^45] [^46] [^47] [^48].

As a densely connected recurrent network with writeable weights, CA3/CA2 is a natural system in which to study the emergence, content and function of replay from a computational perspective. In the present study, we consider different perspectives on replay, including replay of predictive representations and specific past experiences [^49] [^50] [^51] [^52] [^53] [^54], through the lens of generalization. We argue that, in a world where no two experiences are exactly alike, replay consolidates the features and structure of the environment that are preserved across specific experiences, in essence sampling from the animalʼs working model of its world. This conceptual model synthesizes elements of both of these theories with prior work on model-based replay to explain how replay can be useful for guiding future behavior, even though representations from distal past experience that are not imminently behaviorally relevant are sometimes replayed [^54] [^55] [^56]. In support of this conceptual framework, we present three levels of modeling—spiking network, biophysical and abstract normative—reproducing existing experimental findings and demonstrating consolidation of generalizable structure in recurrently connected models of CA3. Central to all three approaches is our major prediction: inhibitory synapses are also remodeled by the spike-time-dependent long-term potentiation (LTP) rule, which enables the statistical structure of the world to be learned dynamically from experience. We then tested this hypothesis using a novel sparse optogenetic cue cell induction approach and found that cells artificially entrained to statistically unpredictable stimuli accumulate inhibition in SWRs. Taken together, our results in this work posit a role for the LTP of inhibitory synapses in the hippocampus in a high-level cognitive function.

## Results

### Network model of CA3 replay

We first built a large, randomly recurrently connected CA3 spiking network capable of internally generating spontaneous replay [^32] [^33]. We simulated an experiment in which the CA3 network was exposed to both spatial input and random distractor stimuli in an online phase during which synaptic weights were learned by sSTDP. Then, in a subsequent offline phase, learned patterns were read out from spontaneous replay (Fig. [1a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1) and ref. [^57]). Our network consisted of 8,000 leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) pyramidal cells (PCs) and 150 interneurons (INs) in Brian 2 (ref. [^58]) with spike-rate adaptation [^32] [^33] [^59], with 50% of the cells assigned to receive place input and a (non-mutually exclusive) 10% receiving stochastic distractor cue input [^57] (Fig. [1b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1)). These neurons were randomly recurrently connected, with a connection probability of 10% between PCs as well as from PCs onto INs and a connection probability of 25% between INs as well as from INs onto PCs ([Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Sec9)).

![Fig. 1: Suppression of distractor stimuli in a random network model of CA3 replay.](https://media.springernature.com/lw685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41593-024-01745-w/MediaObjects/41593_2024_1745_Fig1_HTML.png?as=webp)

Fig. 1: Suppression of distractor stimuli in a random network model of CA3 replay.

The simulated experiment consisted of 40 laps through a 3-m-long environment at constant velocity. Untuned cells fired at a baseline rate of 0.1 Hz (sampled from a homogeneous Poisson process) throughout the belt. The spike trains of place cells were sampled from an inhomogeneous Poisson process, with a position-dependent Poisson rate *λ* (*x*) (maximum 20 Hz) chosen as a circular Gaussian representing the cellʼs tuning curve. The spike trains of distractor-tuned cells were sampled in the same way, with all distractor cells sharing a distractor tuning curve and the distractor appearing stochastically at a randomized location in each lap (Fig. [1f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1), top, yellow arrows). Like excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons in our network may be tuned to place, cue, both or neither, as experimentally reported [^60] [^61]. During this exploration phase, the weights of this network were trained using sSTDP (Fig. [1d,f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1)). Example slices from the trained weight matrices are shown in Fig. [1e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1). A diagonal band of strong weight emerges in the place cell subnetwork (smoothed and sorted by tuning curve peak), indicating that synapses between place cells with adjacent peaks develop strong connections in proportion to the distance between their tuning peaks. Meanwhile, the distractor cell subnetwork becomes strongly recurrently coupled under the same sSTDP rule (Fig. [1e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1), right), as the distractor cells are simultaneously activated by the same stimulus.

To simulate the offline state (Fig. [1f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1), green highlight and inset), PCs were driven with random Poisson spiking input irrespective of their distractor, place or untuned status, simulating unstructured input from mossy fibers (MFs) (Fig. [1c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1)). We found that place sequences encountered during learning are spontaneously replayed in the forward and backward directions offline in our network (Fig. [1f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1)). Furthermore, we found that the distractor-tuned neurons, despite being generally active in the offline period, are selectively suppressed during spontaneous ripples (Fig. [1g](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1)). This is true of both exclusive distractor cells and cells tuned to both distractors and place, consistent with experimental findings (Supplementary Fig. [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). Spontaneous replay in the network was robust to variations in the exact *I*  →  *E* plasticity rule used, so long as the plasticity rule was a Hebbian LTP rule operating on a timescale of roughly 3 ms (Supplementary Figs. [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1) and [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)).

In summary, we show that replay spontaneously emerges in a randomly recurrently connected network trained on a simulated random cue-spatial (RC-S) task using sSTDP of excitatory and inhibitory synapses. We further show that, even though this replay is completely spontaneous and internal to the network, with no explicit externally applied ‘replay signalʼ, and the learning rule is strictly local to individual synapses, the network self-organizes distributed and specific suppression of cue cells during replay epochs.

### Biophysically detailed model of CA3 replay

We next asked whether sSTDP of recurrent *E*  →  *E* and *I*  →  *E* synapses is sufficient to suppress distractor inputs during offline replay in a biophysical model of the hippocampal CA3 network. We modeled CA3 using 260 multi-comparmental and biophysically detailed pyramidal neurons [^62] and 30 INs with biophysically realistic electrophysiological properties [^63] [^64] ([Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Sec9)). During online learning, PCs and INs received input structured into distinct dendritic input streams, consistent with in vivo data [^64] [^65] [^66] [^67] [^68]. Due to the higher computational cost of simulating three-dimensional (3D) biophysically detailed cells, we overrepresented the populations of interest (place and cue cells) to make our simulations more tractable. Specifically, 80% of PCs received spatially structured place and grid input via an MF and medial entorhinal cortical (MEC) pathway, respectively [^69] [^70] [^71]. The remaining 20% of PCs received a distractor input via a lateral entorhinal cortical (LEC) pathway [^71] (Fig. [2a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)). As in Fig. [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1), the PCs receiving distractor input (that is, cue cells) were driven at a randomly selected location during each lap of the linear virtual track. After online learning with the sSTDP rule, place cell firing increased on average to physiologically relevant firing rates (0.264 Hz to 1.087 Hz) and, furthermore, robustly tiled the track (Fig. [2b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)) with little out-of-field firing (Fig. [2c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)).

![Fig. 2: Distractor suppression in a biophysically detailed model of CA3 replay.](https://media.springernature.com/lw685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41593-024-01745-w/MediaObjects/41593_2024_1745_Fig2_HTML.png?as=webp)

Fig. 2: Distractor suppression in a biophysically detailed model of CA3 replay.

To investigate the offline dynamics of cue cells and place cells, we simulated a single ‘online’ lap during which the network received structured input followed by an ‘offline’ state in which the network received only unstructured random inputs (Fig. [2a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)). The ‘online’ lap confirmed that the network retained place cell and cue cell selectivity obtained during training. During the offline state, epochs of high place cell activity spontaneously emerged (Fig. [2e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)). Sequences of place cells with adjacent tuning peaks along the linear track (Fig. [2c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)) were reactivated during these spontaneous chirps (Fig. [2e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)), consistent with spontaneous memory replay. During these replay-like events, cue cells were widely suppressed (Fig. [2h](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)), and overall place and cue cell firing was significantly negatively correlated (Pearson’s *r*  = −0.19, *P*  < 5 × 10 <sup>−12</sup>, *n*  = 1,288 time bins) over the course of 225 s of the simulated offline state (Fig. [2g](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)). Thus, using a data-driven biophysical model of hippocampal CA3 trained with *E*  →  *E* and *I*  →  *E* sSTDP, we found that, consistent with experimental data, memory replay reinstates representations of the structure of the world while suppressing distractor representations.

### Perturbations and predictions from the biophysical network model

Our central synaptic-level prediction is the existence of a Hebbian LTP rule of GABAergic synapses in the hippocampus that is required for the network-level phenomenon of replay and memory consolidation from noisy observations. Here, we performed a series of perturbation simulations to test the hypothesis that inhibitory plasticity is necessary for cue cell suppression and, more generally, world structure learning. Furthermore, the simulations performed here broaden the main findings and generate additional experimentally verifiable predictions that give insight on neural communication across multiple spatial scales.

We first observed that the median inhibitory weight onto cue cells was 38.9% greater than onto place cells (median *w* <sub><i>I</i> → <i>c</i> <i>u</i> <i>e</i></sub>: 0.0063 uS; median *w* <sub><i>I</i> → <i>p</i> <i>l</i> <i>a</i> <i>c</i> <i>e</i></sub>: 0.0046 uS; *P*  = 2.5 × 10 <sup>−187</sup>, one-sided Mann–Whitney *U* -test) (Fig. [3a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)). Therefore, we performed simulated dual patch-clamp recording of IN–PC pairs to measure inhibitory post-synaptic currents (IPSCs) onto PCs and identify the cellular mechanism that enables cue cells to be suppressed during SWRs. PCs were held in voltage-clamp mode with holding voltages ranging from −65 mV to −45 mV in +5-mV increments. We observed that IPSCs onto cue cells (*n*  = 960 measured pairs) were significantly greater (one-sided Mann–Whitney *U* -test, *P*  < 0.0001 for all holding voltages) than IPSCs onto place cells (*n*  = 4,240 measured pairs) (Fig. [3b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)). The IPSCs onto cue and place cells were smallest at holding potentials closer to the chloride reversal potential (*E* <sub><i>r</i> <i>e</i> <i>v</i>,<i>C</i> <i>l</i></sub>  = −65 mV, place: 0.57 ± 0.32 pA; −65 mV, cue: 0.84 ± 0.08 pA) compared to more depolarized holding potentials (−45 mV, place: 1.71 ± 0.97 pA; −45 mV, cue: 2.53 ± 0.22 pA). These simulations predict that sSTDP of GABAergic synapses generates powerful feedforward inhibition onto cue cells (Fig. [3c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)) that may be critical for suppressing cue cells during periods of high place cell activity, such as SWRs. The validity of this inhibitory feedforward motif can be directly tested in future experiments by measuring IPSCs onto place and cue cells from different classes of INs in vivo.

![Fig. 3: Network perturbations and predictions.](https://media.springernature.com/lw685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41593-024-01745-w/MediaObjects/41593_2024_1745_Fig3_HTML.png?as=webp)

Fig. 3: Network perturbations and predictions.

Given the observation of powerful feedforward inhibition onto cue cells, we next predicted that targeted ablation of inhibitory plasticity will impair the network mechanism of adaptive stimulus [^57]. In agreement with the prediction and further supporting the overarching hypothesis that inhibitory plasticity is necessary for world structure learning, models trained with only recurrent *E*  →  *E* plasticity exhibited correlated activity across the cue cell and place cell populations during the offline epoch (Fig. [3d–f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3); Pearson’s *r*  = 0.1, *P*  = 0.71). As a control, networks trained with *E*  →  *E* plasticity only but containing few cue cells (<3% of total PC population) were still able to generate spontaneous replay events in the forward and reverse directions (Supplementary Fig. [4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1) and Supplementary Fig [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1), top row), similar to ref. [^32]. Additional simulations reported that restoring inhibitory plasticity restores the adaptive stimulus mechanism (Supplementary Fig. [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1), bottom row).

To continue testing alternative mechanisms of cue cell suppression, we performed a simulation of the model with *E*  →  *E* and *E*  →  *I* plasticity (Fig. [3g–i](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)). Similar to the model with only *E*  →  *E* plasticity (Fig. [3d–f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)), cue cells and place cells were significantly co-active during SWRs (*r*  = 0.75, *P*  = 6.2 × 10 <sup>−234</sup>). The collective results of Fig. [3d–i](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3) and Supplementary Fig. [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1) support the hypothesis that inhibitory plasticity is necessary for cue cell suppression. Additionally, from this simulation experiment (Fig. [3g–i](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)), we predict that IN firing rates should stay stable during learning, which can be experimentally verified through a multitude of electrophysiological and/or optical methods.

Next, we sought to determine whether place and cue cells emerge in a model configuration where PCs receive different proportions of MEC and LEC. We simulated a variant of model where place cells received 90% of peak MEC strength and 10% of peak LEC strength, relative to the baseline model, and cue cells received 10% of peak MEC strength and 90% of peak LEC strength, relative to the baseline model (Supplementary Fig. [7d–f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). We observed similar place and cue selectivity as in the baseline model during the online phase and qualitatively similar suppression of cue cells during the offline phase (Supplementary Fig. [7e,f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). We further investigated whether a more heterogeneous distribution of PC firing rates would affect our results and simulated model configurations where random log-normally distributed constant depolarizing current was injected in each PC (Supplementary Fig [7a–c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). The log-normal model exhibited behavior very similar to that of the baseline model.

To summarize, we showed that inhibitory plasticity is both sufficient (Fig. [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2)) and necessary (Fig. [3d–i](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)) for cue cell suppression during SWRs. Additionally, we provide numerous predictions that can be tested experimentally to gather evidence that such a mechanism exists and plays a critical role in memory consolidation. At the cellular level, inhibitory plasticity from *I*  →  *E* synapses should lead to powerful feedforward inhibition onto cue cells, manifesting in the form of increased IPSCs onto cue cells compared to place cells (Fig. [3a–c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)). Furthermore, because models trained with *E*  →  *E* and *E*  →  *I* plasticity did not result in cue cell suppression, IN firing rates should stay stable during learning (Fig. [3g–i](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)). At the network level, ablating inhibitory plasticity (Fig. [3d–f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)) should result in loss of cue cell suppression that reemerges by restoring inhibitory plasticity (Supplementary Fig. [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). At the behavioral level, this suggests that ablating inhibitory plasticity would hinder an animal’s ability to perform world structure inference, leading to impairment for goal-oriented behavioral tasks. Taken together, these simulations highlight how synaptic-level mechanisms can cascade to affect network function across multiple spatial scales.

### Simplified model and theoretical analysis

We next sought to provide intuitions about how our model generates spontaneous replay and the role that inhibitory plasticity plays in structural learning in the presence of noise.

Consider a maximally abstracted world consisting of a set of ‘schemas’ (that may correspond to physical space, conjunctive context, sensory cues or abstract concepts). For simplicity, suppose that there is a one-to-one correspondence between schemas in the outside world and tuned ‘concept neurons’ in the brain, which we represent with colored circles in Fig. [4a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4). Also suppose that a ‘synapse’ exists between any pair of distinct neurons (Fig. [4a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4), weight matrix). The generalizable structure to be learned is simply some sequence of schemas.

![Fig. 4: Abstract schematic of generalizable learning in the presence of noise.](https://media.springernature.com/lw685/springer-static/image/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41593-024-01745-w/MediaObjects/41593_2024_1745_Fig4_HTML.png?as=webp)

Fig. 4: Abstract schematic of generalizable learning in the presence of noise.

To learn this structure, the network modifies the synaptic weight matrix, *W*, in response to observations drawn from a generative process parameterized by the ground truth sequence.

In an ideal world, observations consist only of repeated sequential presentations of the ground truth sequence. In this case, a Hebbian LTP rule on only excitatory synapses is sufficient to learn this sequence: every time a transition *a*  →  *b* is observed, weights *W* <sub><i>a</i> <i>b</i></sub> and *W* <sub><i>b</i> <i>a</i></sub> are both incremented by 1. This process potentiates weights along the diagonal between cells representing adjacent elements of the sequence (Fig. [4a](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4), right; compare to Fig. [1e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1), left). In the offline state, the network receives random input, which may by chance excite an arbitrary neuron *x* <sub>1</sub> in the network. Without loss of generality, we let *x* <sub>1</sub> be a one-hot vector (a vector of length *N* with 1 in the position corresponding to the active neuron and 0 everywhere else) at the first schema in the sequence. We then run the network for *K* timesteps (where *K* is the number of schemas in the sequence), letting the network follow its internal dynamics *x* <sub><i>t</i></sub>  =  *σ* (*W* *x* <sub><i>t</i> −1</sub>), where *σ* is a stochastic nonlinearity that returns a one-hot vector whose non-zero element represents the next active neuron, with probabilities proportional to the *x* <sub><i>t</i> −1</sub> -th column of *W* ([Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Sec9)). Adaptation in the models of Figs. [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1) and [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig2) gives sequences ‘momentum’ by preventing network reactivations from sticking in place or doubling back; to model adaptation abstractly, for each event we use only either the lower half-diagonal of *W*, corresponding to the forward weight matrix *W* <sub><i>F</i> <i>W</i> <i>D</i></sub>, or the upper diagonal, corresponding to the reverse weight matrix *W* <sub><i>R</i> <i>E</i> <i>V</i></sub>. The output sequence is an offline statistical estimator of the ground truth sequence, which we term REPLAY.

A more realistic observation model contains noise: distractor observations may appear stochastically at different locations in the sequence. Using the previous Hebbian LTP rule, when distractor schemas are observed, off-diagonal weights are potentiated in the weight matrix (Fig. [4b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4), right; compare to Fig. [1e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig1), right); these off-diagonal weights create artificial ‘side paths’ through the network that do not follow the world structure path. Because the stochastic distractor can appear before or after any other schema, it eventually creates potential paths between any two observed schemas in the network as well as cycles at replay time. Because REPLAY samples the weight matrix, aberrant paths in the network translate into replay of disorganized, out-of-order sequences (see also [Supplementary Note 1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)).

To rescue replay in this network, it is sufficient to simply inhibit the distractor neuron (orange) at replay time (Fig. [4c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4), top). If distractor neurons are not activated, side paths through the network are blocked, and the network dynamics would simply return a walk through the structure of the world. How does the network select only those neurons corresponding to distractors for inhibition compared to neurons encoding part of the structure of the world? We predict that, in the online period, the network must represent all schemas because it cannot know a priori which schemas will be part of the structure of the world and which will turn out to be distractors. Instead, it must learn selective suppression of schemas that turn out to be distractors, motivating the need for inhibitory plasticity.

To understand why inhibition accumulates on distractor neurons, consider the population firing rate as the mouse navigates the linear track. Because place cells tile the track, the average population firing rate will be near uniform in the absence of any distractor input. When a distractor input is received by the network, the additional excitatory currents will directly drive both PC and IN populations (see Supplementary Tables [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1) – [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1) for connectivity profiles), causing a local increase in the population firing rates for both cell types relative to the baseline condition of only structured input. As the distractor input moves from lap to lap, the network sees this local increase in firing rate among both PC and IN populations at different regions of the track (Fig. [4d](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4)). This local increase compresses the spike timings of PCs and INs during distractor input, increasing the rate in change of inhibitory weight onto PCs (Fig. [4e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4)). This phenomenon potentiates the weights of inhibitory afferents to both place and distractor cells; however, because, at each lap, a different subset of place cells will be active during the distractor input, the inhibition onto the place cell population is distributed. In contrast, total inhibitory weight onto cue cells is increased in every trial as the same population of cue cells will be activated (Fig. [4f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig4)).

Simulations from our detailed biophysical model support this accumulation of inhibition onto cue cells: we found that inhibitory weights and IPSCs onto cue cells are significantly higher than onto place cells (Fig. [3a–c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig3)). This observation suggests that the circuit self-organizes to generate powerful feedforward inhibition onto cue cells, which is sufficient to suppress the effect of distractor inputs during electrophysiological events, such as ripples.

### Experimental verification of model predictions

Finally, we sought to test predictions of our model experimentally. We aimed to test our major prediction that representations with unpredictable statistics accumulate inhibition and are suppressed during replay as a result. Our strategy was to optogenetically implant a sparse population of artificial cue cells into the network in the online phase, subsequently examining whether these artificial cue cells become suppressed in the offline phase. We carried out experiments with direct optogenetic stimulation of sparse subsets of PCs either paired with random sensory cue presentation or without cue presentation (Fig. [5a–d](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig5)). In agreement with our model predictions, we found that cue presentation paired with PC stimulation was sufficient to induce suppression of optogenetically stimulated PCs during SWRs (Fig. [5e,f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig5) and Supplementary Fig. [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). Furthermore, optogenetic PC stimulation unpaired with sensory stimulation was not sufficient to induce SWR suppression; instead, similar to our original observation for cue-responsive CA3 PCs in Terada et al.[^57], these cells showed increased SWR-related recruitment (Fig. [5e,f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig5) and Supplementary Fig. [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)). Together, these results imply that sensory input-driven pre-synaptic inhibition and excitatory post-synaptic activation are both necessary to induce correlative Hebbian plasticity of inhibitory inputs onto the PCs.

We subsequently asked whether distractor suppression through inhibitory plasticity is a general phenomenon that is present outside of CA3/CA2, including circuits without strong excitatory recurrent connectivity, because our model predicts that the locus of plasticity for distractor suppression is at the *I*  →  *E* synapse. In agreement with this prediction, we found that random cue presentation paired with optogenetic stimulation of PCs in hippocampal area CA1 also led to suppressed recruitment of paired PCs (Fig. [5a–f](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig5)) and increased recruitment of unpaired PCs during SWRs (Supplementary Fig [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)) in CA1, similar to our previous findings in CA3/CA2 (ref. [^57]). These results demonstrate that distractor suppression is present in hippocampal circuits with different levels of excitatory connectivity.

Lastly, these experiments also probed whether plasticity alone is sufficient to produce de novo feature selectivity. Although it is known that PCs receive multiple input streams, it may still be the case that cells must be developmentally wired to become cue cells. However, we found that stable cue responses can be entrained onto arbitrary PCs via optogenetic stimulation during cue presentation (Fig. [5d](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#Fig5)). To our knowledge, this is the first time that non-spatial representations have been artificially induced in hippocampal PCs. These results provide support to the conceptual model that PCs likely receive a rich set of place and sensory cue inputs, and feature selectivity is acquired through rapid plasticity of a subset of these inputs.

## Discussion

The precise content of replay, and its role in learning, remains one of the main open questions in the study of the hippocampus. Replay has thus far primarily been studied in highly regular and deterministic experimental environments. However, repeated exposures to the same environment in the real world are not the same: although natural environments contain statistical regularities, these regularities are obscured in imperfect or incomplete observations. A fundamental challenge of learning in the real world is abstracting generalizable, low-dimensional representations for long-term storage amid a continuous barrage of noisy sensory input [^55]. Here we show that a spike-time-dependent LTP rule at CA3 GABAergic synapses is sufficient to enable consolidation of the statistical structure of the world from noisy experience. Using a three-pronged approach of spiking network, biophysical and normative modeling, we demonstrate that this mechanism is biologically plausible and interpretable and reproduces experimental findings. Our model makes the prediction that a ‘test neuron’ with an artificially induced, statistically unpredictable firing field will accumulate inhibition, which we verified using a sparse optogenetic induction strategy. Finally, we simplified the intuitive notion of ‘statistical learning’ by the hippocampus into a statistical inference problem that must be solved using biological primitives, and we argue mathematically that the sSTDP-based biological algorithm that we propose correctly solves this inference problem in the asymptotic limit of observations.

We think that the model-based replay framework we propose here illustrates that the specific experience and predictive/planning representation viewpoints of replay are complementary. According to our conceptual model, replay samples from the animal’s working model of the generalizable structure of its world, synthesizing elements of both of these theories to explain how replay can be useful for planning [^53] [^54], even though representations from distal past experience, which are not imminently behaviorally relevant, are sometimes replayed [^49] [^50] [^51] [^52]. Our results resonate with the findings of a high-level normative study by Sun et al.[^72], which also highlighted generalizability as a possible principle underlying the selection of memories for consolidation. The ability to draw samples from a distribution is computationally powerful, as it enables downstream circuitry to compute arbitrary queries on the underlying model. For instance, these samples are sufficient for a downstream brain area to implement the simplest model-based reinforcement learning strategy, random shooting [^73], with minimal complexity. We note also that this framework is agnostic to the semantics of the replayed representations and, thus, has explanatory and predictive power irrespective of whether the underlying experience consists of spatial, auditory, social or more general abstract sequences and relationships. Our plasticity rule simply embeds the topology of a world into the weight matrix of a randomly recurrently connected network. The networks in our simulations were presented with one-dimensional (1D) sequences, mirroring the sequential nature of experience in any environment: for a two-dimensional (2D) or more complex environment, the world must still be experienced incrementally, through sequential trajectories through it. Recent work has shed light on the spontaneous organization of ‘schemas’ in the hippocampus [^74] [^75] [^76]; our optogenetic manipulations here provide the first evidence, to our knowledgee, that non-spatial schemas can also be acquired in a similar way. Once such representations are organized, the *A*  →  *B* relationships between them can be learned incrementally even if gross sequences are not repeated. Such a learning rule allows previously unobserved sequences to be hypothesized and replayed in the brain. Our model makes the prediction that world structures that are fundamentally non-spatial, corresponding to arbitrary relational rules obeying a well-defined distance metric, can also be learned and replayed by the hippocampus via this mechanism.

Work on Hebbian learning rules in the cortex has focused primarily on glutamatergic synapses onto principal cells [^36] [^77]. Plasticity at the GABAergic synapse has gained greater recognition [^78], although whether the predominant effect is potentiation [^38] [^39], depression [^48] [^79] [^80] [^81] [^82] [^83] [^84] or some combination [^85] requires clarification in vivo. Compellingly for our work here, D’amour and Froemke [^86] showed a symmetric spike-time-dependent LTP rule for the potentiation of inhibitory synapses in auditory cortex. Our results indicate that the phenomenon of replay generalization is robust for a family of inhibitory LTP rules (Supplementary Figs. [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1) and [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)), providing further support for the biological plausibility of this rule.

A limitation of this work is that we treated INs as a uniform population, whereas, in reality, distinct subtypes of INs play very different computational roles in the hippocampus [^60] [^87] [^88] [^89] [^90]. The size of the IN population required to achieve this effect in a spiking network of thousands of neurons is comparatively small; therefore, it is possible that the inhibitory plasticity mechanism that we describe is localized to a particular subtype. Our model predicts that the relevant IN population will respond to cues during exploration and will be recruited to SWRs at rest. Dissecting the subtypes shaped by this inhibitory LTP rule represents an important avenue for future experimental work. A prerequisite of our model is that a subset of GABAergic INs in CA3 exhibits activation by sensory stimulation but stable SWR recruitment (as the locus of plasticity is at the GABAergic synapse). Indeed, in Vancura et al.[^90], we recently identified the cholecystokinin-expressing INs as one such candidate population (see Extended Data Fig. 9 in Vancura et al.[^90]).

Our models are inspired by the recurrent CA3 circuitry, but the mechanisms described here are likely to extend to the recurrent circuitry of area CA2 as well, where SWRs can be initiated [^26]. Furthermore, our experimental results demonstrate that distractor suppression is also present in area CA1, which contains far fewer recurrent collaterals compared to CA3/CA2, and where most replay studies have been performed to date [^30] [^53] [^91]. These results suggest that distractor suppression through inhibitory plasticity generalizes to other hippocampal subregions. Future experiments may explore whether this represents a general coding principle that extends beyond the hippocampus.

Our experiments focused on awake replay [^91], but our model may be extensible to replay occurring during sleep [^53] [^92]. Several differences have been highlighted between awake and asleep replay: awake replays occur in both the forward and backward directions and more faithfully recapitulate experience, whereas sleep replays are biased to the forward direction and are noisier [^92]. These differences may be driven by differences in input between arousal states: in the awake animal, spatially localized sensory input has been suggested as a major contributor to localized awake replay—in particular, reverse replay starting from the animal’s current location [^14] [^15] [^93] or once the animal arrives at a reward [^50] [^51] [^94] [^95] [^96] —whereas hippocampal inputs may be more stochastic during sleep. It has been proposed that these differences make awake replay more suited to planning and sleep replay to memory consolidation [^92]. For simplicity, we simulate replay with random input in the present study, but injecting location-specific offline input biases replay starts to that location, consistent with experimental observations [^14] [^15] [^93], suggesting that simple modifications of our model may lend themselves easily to studies of replay in these distinct states.

Abnormalities in hippocampal neural reactivation, SWRs and inhibitory transmission have been demonstrated in a variety of neuropsychiatric disorders, including temporal lobe epilepsy, schizophrenia and autism [^97] [^98] [^99] [^100] [^101] [^102] [^103] [^104] [^105]. In the present work, we identified a potential link between abnormalities of inhibitory synaptic transmission and memory deficits both in general. As our mechanism links the plasticity of inhibitory synapses to the high-level learning of world structure, any disruption to inhibitory plasticity may contribute to the cognitive deficits observed in this disorder and others [^106] [^107]. Electroencephalography (EEG) signatures of epilepsy have also been associated with the engagement of highly coupled pathological microcircuits [^108] [^109] [^110]; a potential ‘pathological transformation’ is hypothesized to occur from physiological sharp-wave ripples to interictal epileptiform discharges [^111]. We speculate that these pathological coupled ensemble events may be the result of a failure of inhibition onto irrelevant representations during replay.

We provide a mechanistic narrative of how the hippocampus may incrementally assemble a generalized representation of an environment despite only ever receiving noisy observations. The concurrent demands of expressiveness and flexibility provide insight into why a plasticity mechanism, in particular one of inhibitory synapses, is necessary for robust learning in the face of distractors—to wit, the brain cannot know a priori which stimuli are important until it has sufficiently sampled the world. The same stimulus that is a distractor in one context may be essential to understanding the structure of the world in the next. Therefore, the hippocampus must be able to robustly represent all stimuli during online learning as they are received and then selectively consolidate only those representations that model the conserved underlying structure of the ensemble of its experience. We make several novel, experimentally approachable predictions using this framework that will hopefully provide insight into the low-level mechanisms underlying high-level cognitive functions, such as memory consolidation and generalization. We think that the framework we present here provides a bridge between the synaptic and the cognitive levels of our understanding of learning and, if experimentally validated, could shed light on the fundamental question of how distributed, local learning rules can structure initially random neuronal networks to perform highly complex cognitive tasks.

## Methods

### Experimental methods

All experiments were conducted in accordance with National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines and with the approval of the Columbia University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Imaging experiments were performed on 2–4-month-old, heterozygous adult male and female wild-type mice (C57BL/6J) from The Jackson Laboratory (strain no. 000664). Mice were housed on a 12-h light/dark cycle in groups of 2–5 mice (temperature, 22–23  <sup>∘</sup> C; humidity, 40%) and individually after surgery for implantation. Experiments were performed during the dark portion of the cycle.

### Viruses

We used GCaMP8m (pGP-AAV-syn-jGCaMP8m-WPRE, [https://www.addgene.org/162375/](https://www.addgene.org/162375/)), Cre-dependent ChRmine (pAAV-Ef1 *α* -DIO-ChRmine-mScarlet-WPRE, [https://www.addgene.org/130998/](https://www.addgene.org/130998/)) and Cre under hSyn promoter (pENN.AAV.hSyn.Cre.WPRE.hGH, [https://www.addgene.org/105553/](https://www.addgene.org/105553/)). For sparse excitatory opsin labeling, we diluted Cre virus with sterilized saline (1:2,000) and co-injected the virus mixtures containing equal amounts of the three individual rAAV constructs above.

### Surgical procedure

Viral delivery to hippocampal area CA1 and CA2/3 and implantation of headposts and imaging cannula were described previously [^57] [^75] [^90]. In brief, 2–4-month-old mice were anesthetized under isofluorane and placed into a stereotaxic apparatus. Meloxicam and bupivacaine were administered subcutaneously to minimize discomfort. Using a Nanoject syringe, viruses were delivered by stereotactically injecting 50 nl (10-nl pulses) of rAAV at multiple injection sites in left dorsal CA1 (−2.1 mm AP; −1.5 mm ML; −0.9 mm, −1.05 mm and −1.2 mm DV relative to bregma) or CA2/3 (−1.35 mm AP, −1.6 mm ML, −2.1 mm, −1.9 mm DV and −1.6 mm AP, −1.9 mm ML, −2.2 mm, −2.0 mm DV relative to bregma). The mice were allowed to recover in their home cage for 3 d before the window/headpost implant. They were then surgically implanted with a custom metal headpost along with an imaging cannula (diameter, 3.0 mm; height, 1.5 mm for CA1 and 2.0 mm for CA2/3) over the injection locations. In brief, mice were anesthetized under isofluorane and placed into a stereotaxic apparatus. The skull was exposed after subcutaneous administration of meloxicam and bupivacaine. A 3-mm craniotomy was done over the left dorsal hippocampus, centered between the injection coordinates. Dura and cortical layers were gently removed under visual guidance while flushing with ice-cold cortex buffer. The imaging cannula was inserted through the surgical opening in the skull. Finally, the metal headpost was affixed to the skull with dental cement. The mice received a 1.0-ml subcutaneous injection of saline and recovered in their home cage while heat was applied. The mice were monitored for 3 d after the operation until behavioral training began.

### Histology and confocal imaging

After the completion of imaging experiments, mice were transcardially perfused with 20 ml of ice-cold PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by 20 ml of ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, washed with PBS three times for 10 min each and coronally sectioned at a thickness of 100 μm using a vibratome (Leica, VT1200S). Sections were blocked with 10% donkey serum and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 60 min and incubated with a rabbit primary antibody against PCP4 (1:500, HPA005792, Sigma-Aldrich) in the blocking solution for 16 h at 4  <sup>∘</sup> C. Sections were washed three times for 10 min with PBS and incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 donkey secondary antibody against rabbit IgG (1:500, 711-605-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch) in the blocking solution for 6 h at room temperature. After being washed three times for 10 min each in PBS, the sections were incubated with 1 μg ml <sup>−1</sup> DAPI in PBS including 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. The sections were then washed three times for 5 min each in PBS at room temperature. Finally, sections were mounted on glass slides in Fluoromount-G aqueous mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and coverslipped. A Nikon A1 confocal microscope was used to acquire multichannel fluorescence images of the immunolabeled tissue sections; 405-nm, 488-nm, 561-nm and 640-nm laser lines were used for excitation. Each channel was acquired sequentially with a ×10/0.45-NA Plan Apo objective (Nikon) at ×1.0 zoom; 2,048 × 2,048 pixel images were acquired every 3 μm through the entire depth of the tissue sections. The resulting four-channel z-stacks were viewed in ImageJ (NIH).

### Behavioral paradigm with closed-loop optogenetic stimulation

Mice were head fixed above a low-friction, lightweight running wheel [^76] in a behavioral context constructed similarly to the random cue without tactile cue condition (RC-NS) in Terada et al.[^57]. An odor cue was presented pseudo-randomly for 10 trials. Each presentation lasted 1 s, and presentations were separated by a pseudo-random inter-stimulus interval of 40–60 s. The odorant R-(−)carvone (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in mineral oil was passed through tubing in front of the mouse’s snout through a mineral oil-infused stream of compressed air that was evacuated by vacuum at the same speed it was delivered to avoid prolonged exposure. For the first five trials, optogenetic photostimulation was triggered by onset of odor presentation and sustained until odor removal. For the next five trials, odor presentation was conducted without photostimulation to test the induced cue response of the stimulated cells. In the control session, photostimulation alone was delivered at random 40–60-s intervals for five trials. The pre and post 15-min imaging sessions were conducted to monitor SWR-related dynamics of the stimulated cells before and after the stimulation sessions. Animal locomotion behavior (active running versus passive rest) was monitored via velocity.

### Identification of optogenetic-evoked response, induced cue selectivity and SWRs

#### Stimulated cell detection

A trial-averaged fluorescence peri-stimulation time histogram (PSTH) was computed for each cell for odor presentations paired with photostimulation and without photostimulation. The principal components were extracted from the PSTHs of all imaged cells. We confirmed each first principal component represented cell activation in response to cue presentation (whether optogenetically evoked or induced selectivity). We carried out *k* -means clustering in the space of *P* *C* 1 <sub><i>s</i> <i>t</i> <i>i</i> <i>m</i></sub> versus *P* *C* 1 <sub><i>u</i> <i>n</i> <i>s</i> <i>t</i> <i>i</i> <i>m</i></sub> to classify the PCs based on ‘stimulated versus unstimulated’ and ‘odor responding versus non-responding’ factors (Supplementary Fig. [8b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1)).

#### Detection and comparison of SWR-associated dynamics

We smoothed and averaged (five-bin window, Gaussian filter) the Δ *F* / *F* <sub>0</sub> values of unstimulated cells to identify candidate highly synchronous events (HSEs) associated with SWRs in CA1 and CA2/3. Frames associated with locomotion were removed, and HSEs were defined as transients on the population average that exceeded the mean by at least 3 s.d. Then, peri-SWR-HSE PSTHs of the stimulated cells were calculated from the pre-rest and post-rest sessions, concatenated to extract principal components in each condition (Cue-STIM and Random) and subarea (CA1 and CA2/3). We confirmed that the first principal components of all groups represented SWR-associated transients as previously reported [^57]. Finally, we compared these SWR component coefficients between pre-rest and post-rest sessions within the stimulated cells.

### Spiking network model

We first built a random network model of a CA3 subnetwork capable of generating spontaneous replay, similar to previous work [^32] (see reference for detailed equations and constants). All network simulations were run in Brian 2 (ref. [^58]).

#### Neurons

We initialized the network with 8,000 PCs and 150 INs. Each neuron was modeled by a variant LIF model with cellular adaptation [^32] [^59].

$$
{C}_{m}\frac{dV}{dt}=-{g}_{L}(V-{V}_{rest})+{g}_{L}\Delta T\exp \left\{\frac{V-{V}_{thr}}{\Delta T}\right\}-I(t)-w
$$

(1)

$$
{\tau }_{w}\frac{dw}{dt}=a(V-{V}_{rest})-w
$$

(2)

where *C* <sub><i>m</i></sub> is membrane capacitance; *g* <sub><i>L</i></sub> is the leak conductance; *V* <sub><i>r</i> <i>e</i> <i>s</i> <i>t</i></sub> is the resting potential; *V* <sub><i>t</i> <i>h</i> <i>r</i></sub> is the spiking threshold; Δ *T* is a shape parameter; *w* is the adaptation current; and *I* is the input (synaptic) current. When *V* reaches the threshold *V* <sub><i>t</i> <i>h</i> <i>r</i></sub>, a spike is recorded, and *V* is reset to a hyperpolarized reset potential, *V* <sub><i>r</i> <i>e</i> <i>s</i> <i>e</i> <i>t</i></sub>, where it is held for a refractory period, *t* <sub><i>r</i> <i>e</i> <i>f</i></sub>. Adaptation strength is given by *a*.

#### Synapses

Synapses were modeled as double-exponential time-varying conductances

$$
g(t)=\omega A\big(\exp (-t/{\tau }_{d})-\exp (-t/{\tau }_{r})\big)
$$

(3)

where *ω* is the weight of the synapse, and *τ* <sub><i>r</i></sub>, *τ* <sub><i>d</i></sub> are rise and decay time constants. The post-synaptic current was modeled with excitatory and inhibitory components as in ref. [^32] as

$$
I(t)={g}_{AMPA}(t)\big(V(t)-{E}_{exc}\big)+{g}_{GABA}(t)\big(V(t)-{E}_{inh}\big)
$$

(4)

where *E* <sub><i>e</i> <i>x</i> <i>c</i></sub>  = 0, *E* <sub><i>i</i> <i>n</i> <i>h</i></sub>  = −70 mV and *g* <sub><i>AMPA</i></sub> and *g* <sub><i>GABA</i></sub> refer to conductance through postsynaptic AMPA and GABA channels, respectively.

#### Network topology

Let ${\mathcal{I}}$ be the set of inhibitory neurons and ${\mathcal{E}}$ be the set of pyramidal neurons in the network. A random network topology was selected by sampling a directed Erdös–Rényi graph *G* (8000, 0.1) [^112]: that is, a synapse between each (ordered) pair of pyramidal neurons $(i,j)\in {\mathcal{E}}\times {\mathcal{E}}$ exists with probability 0.1 (drawn i.i.d.). The *I*  →  *E* topology was determined using a similar process: each ordered pair $(i,j)\in {\mathcal{I}}\times {\mathcal{E}}$ exists as a synapse in the network with probability 0.25.

#### Training phase

In the online phase, the network receives a series of observations and modifies its synaptic weights accordingly to estimate the ‘world structure’ sequence that gave rise to those observations. The simulated behavior trace was generated as a simulated animal moving at constant velocity through a circular 1D track.

Simulated spike trains were sampled from a Poisson process with a baseline firing rate of *λ*  = 0.1 Hz.

A random 50% of PCs were selected as place cells and received spatially tuned input in a pre-allocated place field covering 10% of the track, tuned such that the mean firing rate in the center of its place field was 20 Hz. The firing rate *λ* <sub><i>i</i></sub> (*x*) of cell *i* at each spatial position *x* is given by

$$
{\lambda }_{i}(x)={\lambda }_{max}\exp \left(-\frac{{(x-{\mu }_{i})}^{2}}{2{\sigma }^{2}}\right)
$$

(5)

where *μ* <sub><i>i</i></sub> is the peak of cell *i* ’s tuning curve and *σ* parameterized.

A random 15% of neurons were chosen to be ‘cue cells’, consistent with findings by Terada et al.[^57]. A cue input was presented at a random location at each lap. The firing rate *λ* (*x*) of all cue cells at each spatial position *x* on lap *k*

$$
\lambda (x)={\lambda }_{max}\exp \left(-\frac{{(x-{x}_{k})}^{2}}{2{\sigma }^{2}}\right)
$$

(6)

where *x* <sub><i>k</i></sub> corresponds to the location of cue presentation on lap *k*

Spikes within a 5-ms refractory period of a previous spike were rejected.

An sSTDP rule was used to train the network as follows. For each synapse *i*  →  *j* with weight *w*, for each pair of spikes

$$
\Delta w=A\exp \left(-\frac{| {t}_{post}-{t}_{pre}| }{\tau }\right)
$$

(7)

This rule was used at both excitatory and inhibitory synapses, with different values of *τ* <sub><i>E</i></sub>, *τ* <sub><i>I</i></sub>.

To explore the space of inhibitory plasticity rules, we examined the family of spike-time-dependent plasticity rules of the form [^59]

$$
\Delta w=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}{A}_{+}\exp \left\{-\frac{\Delta t}{{\tau }_{+}}\right\},\quad &\,\text{if}\,\Delta t > 0\text{}.\\ {A}_{-}\exp \left\{-\frac{\Delta t}{{\tau }_{-}}\right\},\quad &\,\text{if}\,\Delta t\le 0\text{}.\end{array}\right.
$$

(8)

where Δ *w* <sub>+</sub> is applied to the synapse if *t* <sub><i>p</i> <i>r</i> <i>e</i></sub>  <  *t* <sub><i>p</i> <i>o</i> <i>s</i> <i>t</i></sub> (causal spiking), and Δ *w* <sub>−</sub> is applied to the synapse if *t* <sub><i>p</i> <i>r</i> <i>e</i></sub>  >  *t* <sub><i>p</i> <i>o</i> <i>s</i> <i>t</i></sub> (anti-causal spiking). We varied the parameters *A* <sub>+</sub>, *A* <sub>−</sub>, *τ* <sub>+</sub> and *τ* <sub>−</sub>. When *A* <sub>+</sub>  =  *A* <sub>−</sub>  > 0, we obtain the standard sSTDP rule. When *A* <sub>−</sub>  = − *A* <sub>+</sub>, we obtain the classic (asymmetric) sSTDP rule. Varying *τ* <sub>+</sub>, *τ* <sub>−</sub> independently while holding *A* <sub>+</sub>  =  *A* <sub>−</sub>  > 0 gave a variety of LTP rules. Throughout this exploration, we held the *E*  →  *E* plasticity rule fixed as the experimentally demonstrated rule (ref. [^29]).

#### Offline (replay) phase

In the offline state, excitatory cells received random low-level (0.1 Hz) Poisson spiking input. Replay emerged spontaneously in the network.

### Biophysical model

The biophysical model of hippocampal CA3 was built from 260 CA3 PCs and 30 fast-spiking perisomatic-targeting INs. Each neuron was represented as a multi-compartment model, where each compartment mapped to various physical locations, including soma, basal dendrites and apical dendrites. Additionally, each neuron contained biophysical ion channel models with realistic conductances, thereby allowing the user to track the continuous intracellular voltage trace within each compartment. PCs and INs received external input from multiple afferent sources modeled as Poisson spike generators, including the MEC, the LEC and the dentate gyrus (DG) via MFs.

The neurons were placed into a virtual CA3 volume divided into stratum lacunosum moleculare (SLM), statrum radiatum (SR), stratum lucidum (SL), stratum pyramidale (SP) and stratum oriens (SO). The soma position of PCs and INs as well as specific connectivity patterns among PCs, INs and external inputs (convergence and specific site of contact) were allocated according to known experimental data. For further details on biophysical parameters, see Supplementary Tables [1–3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-024-01745-w#MOESM1).

#### Training and replay

Similar to the spiking network model, a virtual circular track was generated and simulations were performed with the mouse moving at a constant velocity. All simulations were run in the NEURON environment [^113] with *d* *t*  = 0.1 ms.

For training, 80% of CA3 PCs were selected as place cells and received pre-allocated and spatially tuned input from external MEC and MF sources. Specifically, MFs carried place inputs, whereas MEC afferents carried grid inputs. The diameter of place fields was selected to cover 10% of the track, tuned such that the mean firing rate at the center of the place field was 20 Hz (see spiking network model for equations). Grid inputs were instantiated with similar parameters but were additionally allowed to cover the virtual track in a periodic manner with a grid spacing equal to 10% of the track. The remaining 20% of CA3 PCs were selected as cue cells and received pre-allocated distractor input from external LEC. The distractor input was activated at a random location once per lap.

During the offline epoch, we removed spatially structured and distractor input from all external sources. Instead, CA3 neurons received random Poisson spiking input, and spontaneous replay was observed.

### Quantitative analysis

#### Offline cue–place aggregate analysis

The full offline epoch was divided into non-overlapping bins equating to 100 ms of simulation time. The number of spikes for cue cells and place cells in any given bin was extracted and plotted on a 2D histogram. Pearson’s correlation coefficients and *P* values were computed, and a two-tailed *t* -test was used to determine if the cue and place cell subnetworks were functionally connected (*P*  < 0.05) or not (*P*  > 0.05).

Peri-event time histograms were computed according to the z-scores of the bin spike counts computed for the 2D histograms. Time bins with a place cell z-score of 1 or greater were considered to be occurrences of HSEs. Pearson’s correlation coefficients and *P* values were computed to test for correlation between z-scores of place cell and cue cell activity over time periods starting at 2 s before and ending 2 s after the occurrence of each HSE. Cue cell suppression was quantified as negative correlation with *P*  < 0.05.

### Abstract model

Let ${\mathcal{X}}$ be a finite set of schemas. Define a ‘sequence’ of length *K* as a string *X*  = (*x* <sub>1</sub>, *x* <sub>2</sub>, *x* <sub>3</sub>,..., *x* <sub><i>K</i></sub>) with each ${x}_{i}\in {\mathcal{X}}$ and *x* <sub><i>i</i></sub>  ≠  *x* <sub><i>j</i></sub>, *i*  ≠  *j*. Let ‘world structure’ consist of a ground truth sequence $Y=(\;{y}_{1},{y}_{2},{y}_{3},\ldots ,{y}_{K})\in {{\mathcal{X}}}^{K}$. For simplicity, we consider only forward replay; the analysis for backward replay is identical.

Real observations consist of a mix of fixed elements of the world and experience-specific stimuli. Therefore, we draw *observations* *X* i.i.d. according to the following generative process: for each (1 ≤  *i*  ≤  *K*  − 1), let *x* <sub><i>i</i></sub>  =  *y* <sub><i>i</i></sub> (ground truth) with probability 1 −  *p* and *x* <sub><i>i</i></sub>  =  *z* (distractor) with probability *p*.

$$
{x}_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}{ll}{y}_{i}\quad &{\rm{with}}\,{\rm{probability}}\,1-p\\ z\quad &{\rm{with}}\,{\rm{probability}}\,p\end{array}\right.
$$

(9)

Assume each character $x\in {\mathcal{X}}$ is represented by a single ‘neuron’, and let $W=| {\mathcal{X}}| \times | {\mathcal{X}}|$ be a matrix initialized with zeros representing synaptic weights between these neurons. Assume for the sake of simplicity that the network is fully connected (equivalently, let each model neuron represent the equivalence class of neurons coding for each stimulus).

Let *W* <sup><i>I</i></sup> be another $| {\mathcal{X}}| \times | {\mathcal{X}}|$ matrix representing feedforward inhibitory weights from the subset of inhibitory neurons tuned to inputs onto *x*.

Now we introduce the plasticity rule. Under the simplest model of inhibitory plasticity, symmetric LTP of inhibitory synapses occurs via sSTDP. For each transition *x* <sub><i>i</i></sub>  →  *x* <sub><i>i</i> +1</sub> in an observation, increment ${W}_{{x}_{i}{x}_{i+1}}$ by 1. Increment the inhibitory weight ${W}_{{x}_{i}{x}_{i}}^{\,I}$ by 1 and the weights ${W}_{{x}_{i+1}{x}_{i}}^{\,I},{W}_{{x}_{i}{x}_{i+1}}^{\,I}$ by *β*  < 1, which parameterizes the inhibitory plasticity kernel decay rate.

#### Replay phase

In the offline phase, the network reads out its running estimate of the ‘world structure’ sequence.

Let ${\hat{y}}_{t}$ be a one-hot vector representing the neuron active at time *t* in a replay sequence. Then, $W\hat{y}$ denotes the (unnormalized) distribution of neurons active at time *t*  + 1.

We add the inhibitory term $-\alpha {W}^{\,I}\hat{y}$, where *α* is a parameter controlling the relative strength of inhibition and excitation. As inhibition predominates during replay, we pick *α*  > 1: for instance, *α*  = 1.5. Finally, to model stochasticity in sequence activation, we introduce a stochastic nonlinearity. For a non-negative vector *w*, let the random variable *σ* (*w*) denote the random one-hot vector obtained by drawing a sample from the categorical distribution with probabilities proportional to *w* (if any values of *w* are negative, we replace them with 0).

Hence, we can write the dynamics of the network during replay as:

$$
{\hat{y}}_{t+1}=\sigma\big(\big(W-\alpha {W}^{\,I}\big){\hat{y}}_{t}\big)
$$

The REPLAY estimator. Without loss of generality, let ${\hat{y}}_{0}={y}_{0}$ (that is, the first element in the ground truth sequence). Run this network for *K* timesteps. We define the REPLAY estimator $\hat{Y}$ to be

$$
\hat{Y}=\left(\;{\hat{y}}_{0},{\hat{y}}_{1},\ldots ,{\hat{y}}_{K}\right)
$$

### Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical details of comparisons are specified in either the main text or the figure legends. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous publications [^57]. Mice showing poor imaging windows (low-quality viral expression or scarring), animals that did not display clear SWRs in their local field potential (LFP) signals and those that did not progress along the behavioral paradigms described above were excluded before commencing experiments. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment. Unless otherwise noted, box plots represent median and interquartile range, and whiskers extend to cover the distribution without outliers (defined as points above 1.5× the interquartile range below or above the box edges). For comparisons between two populations, a paired sample or unpaired *t* -test was applied if the data points followed a normal distribution. In the instances where parametric tests were used, distributions were assumed to be normal, but this was not formally tested. To analyze data that were not normally distributed, a Mann–Whitney *U* -test was used. All tests were two-sided unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends; \* *P*  < 0.05; \*\* *P*  < 0.01; \*\*\* *P*  < 0.001. Data analysis was performed and figures were generated using custom-made software in Python 3.8.
