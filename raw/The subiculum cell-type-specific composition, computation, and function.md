## Highlights

- The subiculum, the primary hippocampal output region, exhibits rich excitatory neuron diversity that contributes to organizing information flow to downstream brain regions.
- Integrating molecular, spatial, circuit, and functional data reveals discrete subiculum neuron subtypes with coherent multimodal identities.
- These subtypes form spatially discrete and abutting subdomains that vary in the dorsal–ventral, proximal–distal, and superficial–deep axes.
- Ultimately, these subtypes appear to bias navigation, memory, motivation, and stress signals to distinct projection pathways.
- A cell-type-centered perspective on the subiculum provides a framework for interpreting hippocampal computations and guiding future research.

## Abstract

The hippocampus is a key brain region for understanding the neurobiological mechanisms of learning, memory, and cognition. The subiculum, which provides the predominant form of hippocampal output via excitatory neurons, offers an advantageous perspective with which to understand these mechanisms. In this review, focusing on recent rodent research, we summarize and integrate results on subiculum excitatory neurons across molecular, cellular, circuit, and functional properties. This collective work converges upon discrete excitatory neuron subtypes coherently represented across modalities, with these subtypes occupying distinct spatial subdomains within the subiculum. This subtype framework helps contextualize existing findings on hippocampal processing, illustrates the possibility of spatially restricted parallel hippocampal outputs, and provides a biological roadmap for future experiments pursuing the mechanisms of hippocampal function.

Such an understanding has been greatly facilitated by modern-day tools for cell typing. Technological advances now allow neuronal populations to be identified in data-rich anatomical, molecular, circuit, and functional measurements. For example, single-cell gene expression and transcriptomics permit the discovery of molecular cell types and their organization in space, viral tracing facilitates the study of long-range projections, and *in vivo* recordings permit the widespread recording of neural activity and its relationship to behavior. All of these methods are especially tractable for the rodent subiculum: the broad anatomical extent of the subiculum facilitates spatial mapping of subtypes, the widespread long-range outputs of the subiculum make projections amenable to modern-day viral tracing methods, and its position at the output of the hippocampus makes it uniquely accessible for linking activity to broader circuits and their role in a variety of behaviors. This accessibility allows functional dynamics to be directly related to projection targets and anatomical organization, making the subiculum an ideal system for probing crossmodal coherence in neuronal identity and interpreting hippocampal output with subtype specificity ([Box 1](#b0005)).

## The geometry of the subiculum in the rodent brain

The subiculum is a bilateral brain structure that exhibits complex three-dimensional geometry ([Figure 1](#f0005)). The largest anatomical axis of the subiculum is referred to as the dorsal–ventral or ‘long’ axis, which spans a few millimeters of the rodent brain ([Figure 1](#f0005) A). Cross-sections perpendicular to the dorsal–ventral axis reveal cytoarchitecture associated with the classic trisynaptic loop, wherein the subiculum acts as the hippocampal output following signal transmission through upstream regions from the dentate gyrus to CA3 and CA1 ([Figure 1](#f0005) B,C). Anatomical variation in this two-dimensional section of the subiculum can be described by two complementary axes. The ‘proximal–distal’ (i.e., ‘transverse’) axis spans from neurons adjacent to CA1 (proximal) to those furthest away (distal) ([Figure 1](#f0005) D). The ‘superficial–deep’ (i.e., ‘radial’) axis spans from neurons abutting the white matter (deep) to those at the opposite end of the pyramidal cell layer (superficial) ([Figure 1](#f0005) E). Although these two axes appear to be defined orthogonally, it is important to note that the curvature of their respective reference boundaries (CA1 and white matter) can cause them to vary together across space.

This body of work was a critical first step in identifying that the subiculum is not a cellularly homogeneous structure and, at least at the molecular level, demonstrates discrete subtype-specific heterogeneity ([Figure 2](#f0010) A–F). ## Concluding remarks and future perspectives

The subiculum remains one of the least understood hippocampal subregions. This can be attributed, at least in part, to the complex cytoarchitectural and functional properties of the subiculum relative to other hippocampal subfields. Importantly, this complexity is well-suited to being addressed by modern-day cell-type-specific approaches that can overcome these biological challenges and provide key interpretations of subiculum function ([Figure 3](#f0015)). Coherent integration of molecular identity, physiology, connectivity, and function is a rare opportunity that seems especially tractable in the subiculum and will likely provide results of broad interest across neuroscience. This understanding of the subiculum will be bolstered by a variety of emerging open science tools and datasets that provide cell-type-specific interpretations of the structural and functional properties of the subiculum ([Table 2](#t0010)). In the subsequent paragraphs, and in the ‘Outstanding questions’ box, we highlight several key avenues for discovery that are newly tractable by cell-type-specific approaches.

This suggests the intriguing possibility that the subiculum may flexibly compute distinct operations dependent on behavioral state and need, with the roles of subiculum neuron subtypes within these multiplexed operations being of key importance in future research.

Outstanding questions

Do upstream inputs to the subiculum (e.g., CA1, entorhinal cortex, amygdala, and thalamus) target specific subiculum excitatory neuron subtypes? If they do, how does this input specificity influence information routing and computation?

To what extent does local excitation from recurrent circuits within the subiculum synchronize or segregate activity across subtypes, and how does this shape hippocampal output dynamics?

How are inhibitory neurons embedded within subiculum circuits? To what degree do they control excitatory neuron subtype-specific activity and output?

To what extent are subiculum excitatory neuron subtypes, and their respective properties, conserved across species?
