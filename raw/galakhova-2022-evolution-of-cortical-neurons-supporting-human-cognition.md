## Highlights

- Cortical expansion in humans might be driven by changes in neuronal properties and connectivity rather than sheer increase in neuronal numbers.
- Recent breakthroughs in large-scale transcriptomic analyses of cortical neurons make it now possible to identify the genetic signature of neuron types and compare neuronal properties across species.
- Human upper cortical layers contain neurons with large and complex dendrites that allow a single human neuron to perform computations similar to a multilayered network.
- Strong and reliable synapses that recover quickly from synaptic activity, and fast signaling properties, help these neurons efficiently process information.
- Several neuronal features of human neurons, such as dendritic size and complexity and fast signaling properties, also associate with cognitive ability in human individuals, supporting the link between neurons and cognitive function not only between but also within species.

## Abstract

Human cognitive abilities are generally thought to arise from cortical expansion over the course of human brain evolution. In addition to increased neuron numbers, this cortical expansion might be driven by adaptations in the properties of single neurons and their local circuits. We review recent findings on the distinct structural, functional, and transcriptomic features of human cortical neurons and their organization in cortical microstructure. We focus on the supragranular cortical layers, which showed the most prominent expansion during human brain evolution, and the properties of their principal cells: pyramidal neurons. We argue that the evolutionary adaptations in neuronal features that accompany the expansion of the human cortex partially underlie interindividual variability in human cognitive abilities.

However, human brains are capable of developing unique cognitive skills (e.g., sharing and storing information through symbolic language), without which human civilization would not be possible. For thousands of years, one of the most fundamental questions in philosophy and neuroscience has been the biological origin of human intelligence. What neurobiological principles underlie the cognitive abilities of the human species?

In conclusion, we are only beginning to understand how the broad spectrum of human cognitive abilities relates to neuronal function. New insights indicate that the evolution of human cognition and differences in cognitive ability might be driven by similar mechanisms at primarily the neuronal and microcircuit levels: increased cell size and complexity, more diverse computational repertoire, and distinct expression profiles of various genes within and between neuron types. Identifying conserved and specialized features of cellular organization and function in the human brain and building theories of how they support human cognition is only the first step. The critical and challenging next direction is to formulate testable predictions (see [Outstanding questions](#b0015)). Novel high-resolution techniques applied to human neurons might provide the necessary tools to face this challenge and directly test theories of neurons supporting human cognition.

Outstanding questions

Many comparisons described here are based on rodent to human comparisons, but how do neuronal features compare across primate species? Are there truly human-specific neuron types and features?

How do neuronal features of different neuron types, especially human-specific types, vary across different cortical areas? Are there gradients in gene expression profiles and physiology (similar to gradients in neuron and cortical morphology) from sensory to higher-order association areas?

Can specific neuronal features be manipulated to establish causal relationships between gene expression profiles, specific cellular phenotypes, neuron and network function, and ultimately cognitive function? Until now the relationship between neuronal features and cognitive ability remains correlational.

How do neuronal connectivity motifs within cortex relate to macroscale connectivity? Where are the projection targets of, for example, cortico-cortical layer 3 pyramidal neurons? Where do they receive inputs from?

We only focused here on cortical pyramidal neurons, but cortex contains many more neuronal and non-neuronal types, such as inhibitory interneurons and glia cells. How do the properties of these cells compare across species?

Morphological and physiological diversity within and between neuron types may be caused by differential gene expression. Are there transcriptomic correlates of these specific neuronal features?

What role do specialized neuron types play in human cortex? How do these neurons, such as the large, spindle-shaped Von Economo neurons found in restricted cortical regions of large-brained animals (humans, great apes, macaques, cetaceans, cows, and elephants) contribute to cortical processing? Do the characteristic double-bouquet cells facilitate cortical computation by organizing pyramidal neuron information processing in the primate cortex in ‘microcolumns’?

## Glossary

**Apical**

the main dendrite extending to L1 and the surface of the cortex from neurons located in deeper layers.

**Avian pallium**

the layers of grey and white matter that cover the upper surface of the cerebrum in birds; in mammals pallium is referred to as cerebral cortex.

**Axons**

long projections that originate at the cell body of a neuron and conduct electrical signals (action potentials) to other neurons.

**Cortical (micro)circuit**

two or more synaptically connected neurons that can collectively produce a functional output.

**Dendrites**

tree-like processes of neurons that are specialized in receiving synaptic information through synapses.

**Dendritic excitability**

the readiness of synapses and branches in a dendritic tree to respond with a positive change in membrane potential (depolarization).

**Dendritic (calcium) potential**

depolarization of the dendrites caused by the influx of ions through the activation of voltage-gated channels or receptors.

**Depression**

the progressive reduction of the postsynaptic response.

**Facilitate**

show facilitation, an increase in synaptic efficacy.

**Gigantopyramidal Betz cells**

giant pyramidal neurons located within L5 of the primary motor cortex, the largest pyramidal neurons within cortex.

**Gyrencephalization**

cortical folding.

**Non-allometrically**

deviating from the expected relationship of volume to body mass.

**Presynaptic**

the neuron that releases neurotransmitters.

**Postsynaptic**

the neuron that receives neurotransmitters.

**Pyramidal neurons**

principal excitatory neurons of cerebral cortex and hippocampus.

**Short-term plasticity**

a temporary change in synaptic efficacy caused by the history of presynaptic activity.

**Soma**

cell body.

**Spine**

a small protrusion on the dendritic membrane where synaptic connection (synapse) is formed.

**Synapse**

junctions that allow a neuron to electrochemically transmit a signal from axon of one cell to the dendrite of another cell. Inhibitory synapses decrease the likelihood of firing action potential, while excitatory synapses increase its likelihood.
